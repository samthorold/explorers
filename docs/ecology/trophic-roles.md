# Trophic Roles

Ecology textbooks name three fundamental roles — producer, consumer, decomposer — and often present them as distinct categories of organism. This document argues they are better understood as emergent positions in a flow network defined by two currencies with different physics. Energy flows one way. Matter cycles. The three roles exist because these two constraints, operating simultaneously, require at least three functional positions to sustain a biotic system indefinitely.

## Two currencies, two physics

### Energy: one-way flow

The second law of thermodynamics dictates that every energy conversion is lossy. Solar radiation is captured and converted to chemical bond energy by photosynthesis; that chemical energy is partially converted to work and new biomass at each subsequent transfer; the remainder is dissipated as metabolic heat. Heat at ambient temperature cannot be reconverted to chemical energy by any biological process. Energy therefore flows in one direction through the biotic system: source (sun, chemical substrate) → living system → heat. It cannot cycle. Every joule used is permanently degraded. The energy flow through ecosystems is covered in detail in [energy flow](energy-flow.md).

### Matter: cyclic flow

The conservation of mass means that atoms are never consumed, only relocated. Carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and other elements move between pools — atmospheric, dissolved, mineral, organic — but the total quantity in the system is fixed. A carbon atom in a glucose molecule can become part of a cell wall, be respired as CO2, dissolved in ocean water, taken up by a diatom, and eaten by a copepod, but it is the same atom throughout. Matter can therefore cycle indefinitely, and in a sustained ecosystem it must — because the supply of key nutrients (especially phosphorus) is finite on ecological timescales. The details of nutrient cycling are covered in [nutrient cycling](nutrient-cycling.md).

### The fundamental asymmetry

Every organism needs both energy and matter to grow and reproduce. Energy provides the capacity to do work — to synthesize macromolecules, maintain membrane gradients, move, reproduce. Matter provides the raw materials — the carbon skeletons, the nitrogen for amino acids, the phosphorus for nucleic acids. An organism with energy but no matter cannot grow. An organism with matter but no energy cannot do anything with it.

Because energy flows one way while matter cycles, the biotic system requires continuous energy input from outside (ultimately solar or geochemical) but does not require continuous matter input — provided matter is effectively recycled. This asymmetry is the reason the three trophic roles exist in the configuration they do.

## The energy-acquisition split: autotrophy versus heterotrophy

The most fundamental division among organisms is the answer to a single question: where does your energy come from?

**Autotrophs** capture energy from an abiotic source — solar radiation (photoautotrophs) or reduced inorganic compounds such as hydrogen sulfide, ammonia, or ferrous iron (chemoautotrophs). They convert this abiotic energy into organic molecules, creating new biomass from inorganic precursors. Photoautotrophy dominates global primary production: terrestrial and marine photosynthesis together fix approximately 240 Pg C/year (Field et al. 1998; Beer et al. 2010). Chemoautotrophy is quantitatively minor at the global scale but dominates in specific environments — hydrothermal vents, deep subsurface rock, anoxic sediments — where light is absent but reduced substrates are available (Bach et al. 2006).

**Heterotrophs** cannot capture energy from abiotic sources. They obtain energy by consuming organic molecules that were originally synthesized by autotrophs (or by other heterotrophs in a chain that traces back to autotrophs). Every heterotroph depends, directly or indirectly, on autotrophic energy capture. Heterotrophy is not a lesser strategy — it is the only viable one once the physical constraints (light availability, substrate access) that enable autotrophy are absent. And it is metabolically versatile: heterotrophs exploit a wider range of organic substrates, across a wider range of physical conditions, than any single autotrophic pathway.

This division is metabolic — it concerns the biochemistry of energy capture. But it has profound consequences for how organisms acquire the other currency: matter.

## Nutrient acquisition is coupled to energy strategy

Autotrophs and heterotrophs face different nutrient acquisition problems, and these differences arise directly from their energy strategies.

### Autotrophs: nutrients from abiotic substrate

An autotroph captures energy from light or chemical substrate and builds biomass from inorganic precursors (CO2, H2O, mineral ions). Its nutrients — nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, trace metals — must be extracted from the abiotic environment: soil solution, water column, mineral surfaces. This extraction requires physical infrastructure. Terrestrial plants invest heavily in root systems and mycorrhizal partnerships to access soil nutrients (Smith & Read 2008). Phytoplankton rely on membrane transport proteins and, in nutrient-depleted waters, on upwelling or mixing events to replenish the supply.

The key point: autotrophs get their energy and their matter from different sources. Energy comes from light (or chemical substrate); matter comes from dissolved or mineral pools. The two acquisition processes are decoupled — an autotroph can be energy-saturated but nutrient-limited, or vice versa.

### Heterotrophs: nutrients as a byproduct of energy acquisition

A heterotroph consumes organic matter — biomass or its remains. That organic matter contains both chemical energy (in carbon bonds) and nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, and other elements incorporated into the tissue). When a heterotroph eats, it acquires energy and matter simultaneously from the same source. The two currencies are coupled in the food itself.

This coupling simplifies the nutrient acquisition problem but introduces a different constraint: stoichiometric mismatch. The nutrient ratios in food rarely match the nutrient ratios the consumer needs for its own growth. A herbivore eating plant tissue with a C:N ratio of 40:1 but requiring a body C:N ratio of 6:1 must process enormous quantities of carbon to obtain sufficient nitrogen, excreting the excess carbon as CO2 or fecal matter (Sterner & Elser 2002). This stoichiometric imbalance drives much of the variation in trophic efficiency and is a primary mechanism by which nutrients are recycled back to the abiotic pool — not through decomposition alone, but through the metabolic waste of every heterotroph.

## Consumer and decomposer: same machinery, different targets

Textbooks typically distinguish consumers (organisms that eat living things) from decomposers (organisms that eat dead things). This distinction is real and ecologically important, but it is not a physiological one. Both consumers and decomposers are heterotrophs. Both obtain energy by breaking down organic molecules. Both excrete metabolic waste that returns nutrients to the abiotic pool. The difference is the state of the target.

**Consumers** feed on living biomass. Their targets resist being eaten — through defenses, escape behavior, immune responses, structural barriers. The consumer must overcome these defenses, which imposes energetic costs and drives coevolutionary arms races. Consumer-prey interactions create the trophic cascade dynamics that structure food webs (Paine 1966; Estes et al. 2011).

**Decomposers** feed on dead organic matter. Their targets do not resist. The challenges are different: accessing the substrate physically (penetrating bark, soil aggregates, chitin), breaking down recalcitrant compounds (lignin, cellulose, keratin), and competing with other decomposers for the resource. Fungi and bacteria dominate decomposition not because they possess unique heterotrophic biochemistry but because their growth forms — hyphae that penetrate substrate, extracellular enzyme secretion, high surface-area-to-volume ratios — are well-suited to processing dead, structurally complex organic matter (de Boer et al. 2005). Decomposition effort is itself regulated by need: soil microbes raise production of nutrient-acquiring enzymes when that nutrient is scarce and cut it when the nutrient is supplied directly (Allison & Vitousek 2005). A decomposer does not attack dead matter at a fixed rate.

### Why decomposition falls to specialists

Most dead organic matter is polymer: cellulose, lignin, protein, chitin. These molecules are too large to take into a cell, so a decomposer digests them outside its body. It secretes enzymes into the substrate and absorbs what they release. That arrangement carries costs that favour organisms built for the job:

- **Enzymes are costly.** They are proteins, paid for in carbon and nitrogen before any return arrives. Their cost couples a decomposer's own carbon and nitrogen limitation to how much enzyme it makes (Schimel & Weintraub 2003).
- **Products diffuse away.** Some of what an enzyme releases is lost before its maker can absorb it. The return on enzyme falls with diffusion distance, and depends on the structure of the substrate (Allison 2005).
- **Products are a public good.** A neighbour that makes no enzyme can absorb the released products. Enzyme producers are therefore open to cheaters (Allison 2005).

Hyphae that grow into the substrate, high surface area and enzymes matched to particular substrates all reduce these costs. These are the growth forms of fungi and bacteria described above.

### How efficiently decomposers use dead tissue

A decomposer turns tens of percent of the dead plant tissue it takes up into its own biomass, not a few percent.
- **Microbes.** Microbial carbon-use efficiency (CUE) is the fraction of absorbed carbon that becomes biomass rather than being respired.
  - Thermodynamics caps it at about 0.6.
  - Communities limited by several resources at once are expected to approach about 0.3.
  - Measured means run from about 0.26 to 0.55, depending largely on how CUE is estimated (Sinsabaugh et al. 2013).
  - A later global soil synthesis reports means of 0.34–0.59, again split by method (Hu et al.).
- **Animal detritivores.** Detritivores such as isopods and millipedes assimilate a smaller share of the litter they eat, about 15–30 %. That share is the herbivore range for woody material (see [energy flow](energy-flow.md)). The rest leaves as faeces, which microbes then decompose.

**Litter quality mainly sets the rate.**
- Recalcitrant litter, with high lignin:N, decomposes *slowly* (see [fungi](fungi.md)), but it does not drive the decomposer's efficiency toward zero.
- What the litter's nutrient poverty changes is how much carbon the decomposer respires. Decomposers adjust their CUE to the litter's C:N. They respire more carbon on poor litter so that they release nutrient earlier (see [stability and resilience](stability-and-resilience.md), on Manzoni et al. 2008). Inferred CUE also rises over the decomposition of an N-poor litter cohort as the decomposer's N limitation eases (Manzoni 2017).
- Standard decomposition data cannot tell apart the ways microbes cope with poor litter: flexible CUE, targeted enzymes, changed cell composition and nutrient retention (Manzoni et al. 2021).

**Why the specialist escapes the dissimilarity penalty.**
- For a gut digester, efficiency falls as the food grows chemically unlike the consumer. Carnivores assimilate about 80 % of what they eat, herbivores 20–50 %.
- A decomposer digests outside its body with enzymes matched to its substrate. That is what frees it from this penalty.
- It pays instead in the costs listed above: enzymes it must build, products that diffuse away, and neighbours that absorb what it released. These are an overhead and a limit on rate, not a near-total loss.

*Evidence quality.*
- **The microbial CUE range is well supported.** It rests on several syntheses, and the method dependence is itself a finding of those syntheses. The figures here come from abstracts and secondary summaries; the papers' own tables were not read.
- **The detritivore figure is moderate.** It rests on a few species, an isopod value of about 30 % and the herbivore woody-tissue range. A 456-measurement meta-analysis of isopod and millipede feeding was found, but its pooled assimilation value was not read.
- **The domain does not say whether a decomposer's efficiency is *independent* of how dissimilar its substrate is.** It says only that the efficiency is far higher than a gut digester's on the same food.

**Producers do not mineralise detritus themselves.** Plants take up nutrient mainly in mineral form: ammonium, nitrate and phosphate (Chapin, Matson & Vitousek 2011). Some plants also take up small organic molecules such as amino acids directly (Näsholm et al. 1998). But depolymerising dead matter into those small molecules is microbial work (Schimel & Bennett 2004). Herbivores take only 5–15 % of terrestrial net primary production (Cyr & Pace 1993). Most of the rest enters the detrital pathway, which runs through microbes and detritivores. Where a plant draws on organic nutrient at scale, it does so through a fungal partner (see [fungi](fungi.md), *Mycorrhizal strategy*), not by digesting dead matter itself.

The distinction is not binary. Many organisms span both categories. Vultures are consumers that specialize on dead animals. Parasites consume living tissue from within. Detritivorous invertebrates (earthworms, millipedes, isopods) fragment dead matter, increasing the surface area available to microbial decomposers — a processing role rather than a purely metabolic one. The boundary between "consumer" and "decomposer" is better understood as a continuum of target states than as a categorical divide. The same machinery also does not stop at the species boundary. The nearest living target is often a conspecific, and often a relative. How common that is, and how organisms avoid eating their own kin, is covered in [cannibalism and kin](cannibalism-and-kin.md).

## Body size and the geometry of heterotrophic interactions

Among heterotrophs that target other organisms' biomass, body size ratios between the interacting organisms determine the qualitative character of the interaction. The same fundamental capability — consuming organic matter — produces very different ecological dynamics depending on relative size.

**Grazing.** When the consumer is small relative to its target, the interaction is typically non-lethal. The consumer removes a portion of the target's biomass, and the target survives and regrows. Herbivory on plants is the canonical example: a caterpillar eating leaves, a deer browsing shrubs. The target loses biomass but is not killed (in most cases). Grazers therefore exploit a renewable flow rather than destroying a stock. Cyr & Pace (1993) found that herbivores remove only 5–15% of terrestrial net primary productivity in most ecosystems, leaving the majority to enter the detrital pathway.

**Predation.** When the consumer is large enough relative to its target to kill it, the interaction is lethal and the consumer obtains the entire organism's energy and nutrient content. Predation removes individuals from the prey population, creating top-down regulation. The ecological consequences are qualitatively different from grazing: predation can control prey population size, drive prey behavioral shifts that cascade through ecosystems (Schmitz, Krivan & Ovadia 2004), and structure community composition.

**Scavenging.** When the target is already dead — killed by another predator, senescence, disease, or environmental stress — the interaction is scavenging. The heterotroph consumes dead biomass, functioning as a decomposer of animal tissue. Scavenging occupies the functional space between predation and decomposition. Obligate scavengers are rare (vultures being the notable exception); most scavenging is facultative, performed by predators and omnivores opportunistically (DeVault, Rhodes & Shivik 2003).

These categories grade into each other continuously. A bear fishing for salmon is a predator. The same bear eating a salmon carcass is a scavenger. A hyena killing a wildebeest is a predator; the same hyena driving a lion from its kill is a kleptoparasite; the same hyena eating a carcass it found is a scavenger. The underlying capability — heterotrophic metabolism of animal tissue — is identical across all three interactions. What changes is context: the state and relative size of the target.

## Why three roles are necessary

The three trophic roles are not arbitrary categories. They are the minimum set of functional positions required to sustain an ecosystem given the two-currency constraint.

**Producers** (autotrophs) are necessary because energy enters the biotic system only through abiotic energy capture. Without producers, there is no energy input. No amount of recycling can substitute, because energy does not cycle.

**Consumers** (heterotrophs feeding on living biomass) are not strictly necessary for the system to function — a world of only producers and decomposers is thermodynamically viable. But consumers emerge inevitably wherever living biomass represents a concentrated, accessible energy source, because natural selection favours any organism that can exploit it. Consumers also perform important system-level functions: they regulate producer populations (top-down control), accelerate nutrient turnover through metabolic waste, and create spatial heterogeneity through selective feeding (Schmitz et al. 2010).

**Decomposers** (heterotrophs feeding on dead organic matter) are necessary because without them, the nutrient cycle breaks. Dead organisms lock nutrients in organic form. If those nutrients are not mineralized — converted back to inorganic forms that autotrophs can assimilate — the available nutrient pool shrinks with every death. Production declines as nutrients accumulate in an inaccessible dead pool. Eventually, the system runs out of available nutrients and collapses, even though the total quantity of matter in the system has not changed. Decomposers close the loop. Their ecological necessity is not a unique biochemistry but what their metabolism accomplishes: the return of matter from the dead organic pool to the available inorganic pool.

This can be stated precisely in terms of stocks and flows. At steady state, the rate of nutrient mineralization by decomposers must equal the rate of nutrient incorporation into living biomass. If mineralization falls behind incorporation — because decomposition is slow (cold temperatures, recalcitrant substrates, waterlogged soils) — nutrients accumulate in dead organic matter and production is suppressed. Peatlands, boreal forests, and anoxic sediments are real-world examples of ecosystems where slow decomposition causes massive accumulation of dead organic matter, limiting productivity despite adequate energy input (Clymo 1984; Gorham 1991).

## Trophic roles as network positions

The three roles are better understood as positions in the energy-and-nutrient flow network than as types of organism. A "producer" is any node that converts abiotic energy to organic energy. A "consumer" is any node that obtains energy from living organic nodes. A "decomposer" is any node that obtains energy from dead organic matter and returns nutrients to the abiotic pool.

Some organisms occupy a single position cleanly. Obligate photoautotrophs are pure producers. Obligate saprophytes are pure decomposers. But many organisms span positions. Mixotrophic protists photosynthesize and consume prey simultaneously (Stoecker 1998). Mycorrhizal fungi receive carbon from living plant partners (a consumer-like relationship) while decomposing soil organic matter (a decomposer function). Insectivorous plants photosynthesize (producer) while digesting animal prey (consumer) to obtain limiting nutrients.

In mixotrophs, consumption is expressed conditionally, and the condition is usually the *limiting* currency rather than energy. Light-rich mixotrophic algae increase bacterivory under phosphorus or nitrogen limitation, and eat for nutrient rather than carbon (Nygaard & Tobiesen 1993; Stoecker 1998). The northern pitcher plant (*Sarracenia purpurea*) builds fewer, less carnivorous pitchers when nitrogen is added and more when it is scarce (Ellison & Gotelli 2002). The capacity to consume is retained, but its expression tracks need.

### Mixotrophy: how common, what it eats, what it costs

**On land, producer mixotrophy is rare.** Carnivorous plants number a few hundred species in a handful of lineages (Ellison & Gotelli 2001). They are confined to bright, wet, nutrient-poor habitats such as bogs, seeps and leached sands. Givnish et al. (1984) explain the restriction with a cost/benefit model. Traps cost photosynthetic area and construction, and the nutrient they bring in raises photosynthesis only where light and water are plentiful and soil nutrient is scarce. Everywhere else, roots and mycorrhizae are the cheaper route to nutrient (see [plants](plants.md), *Root allocation and nutrient uptake*). The conditional expression above is the same economy within one plant: *Sarracenia* builds less carnivorous pitchers when nitrogen is added (Ellison & Gotelli 2002).

**In the plankton it is widespread.** Many flagellates and dinoflagellates combine photosynthesis with phagotrophy (Stoecker 1998). In the North Atlantic, small plastid-bearing algae carry out a large share of the grazing on bacteria (Zubkov & Tarran 2008). One reading is that the difference from land follows the same economy: a small cell in nutrient-poor water has no root to grow, and its prey is the nutrient.

**What mixotrophs eat is the limiting nutrient in concentrated form.** Carnivorous plants catch animals, mostly arthropods, whose tissue is far richer in nitrogen and phosphorus than plant tissue. Algal mixotrophs graze bacteria and other cells smaller than themselves. Bacteria are rich in nitrogen and phosphorus relative to their carbon (Sterner & Elser 2002). In neither case is the prey the mixotroph's own kind: carnivorous plants do not catch plants. The plants that do draw on living plants are parasites, such as dodder and mistletoes, which tap a host through haustoria, and that host is usually another species (citation needed).

**Green plants do not feed on litter carbon.** Plants take up amino acids, and some take up protein, as a nitrogen source (Näsholm et al. 1998; Paungfoo-Lonhienne et al. 2008).
- **The carbon that comes with it is small.** It is a "carbon bonus" that lowers the cost of assimilating nitrogen (Franklin et al. 2016). It is about 0.1–0.3 % of tissue carbon in a grass study. The one higher figure, 7–8 %, is *Arabidopsis* fed glutamine on sterile agar.
- **The only plants found taking carbon from litter do so through a fungus, and they are achlorophyllous.** These are orchids that parasitise litter-decaying *Mycena* and *Gymnopus* (Ogura-Tsujita et al. 2009; Martos et al. 2009). The route runs from the litter, through the saprotroph, to the plant. The plant drains the decomposer, not the litter.
- *Evidence quality:* Franklin et al. was read in full text; the others from abstracts. The grass and *Arabidopsis* figures are from secondary summaries. No source was found for a green plant taking a meaningful share of its carbon directly from litter.

**Carrying both machineries costs.** A carnivorous plant's traps are leaves that photosynthesise less, and they cost construction and digestive enzymes. That is the cost side of Givnish et al.'s model, and it is why carnivory pays only in a narrow band of habitats. For plankton mixotrophs, the usual expectation is that a cell carrying both apparatuses does less well at each mode than a specialist in it, and wins where neither mode alone suffices (citation needed).

The roles are emergent properties of the flow network, not innate properties of the organisms. An organism's trophic role is determined by what it does — which flows it participates in — not by what it is. And those flows are constrained, ultimately, by the two physics: energy flows one way, matter cycles, and any self-sustaining biotic system must have functional positions that together maintain both flows.

### A decomposer lineage stays a decomposer

A saprotrophic fungus's lineage cannot cross with plants, and it has no short route to photosynthesis of its own. The saprotroph's own life history (ageing, upkeep, reproduction) is in [fungi](fungi.md), *The saprotroph's life history*.

**Isolation from plants is categorical.**
- **Fungi and plants are about as far apart as two eukaryotes can be.** Fungi are opisthokonts, closer to animals than to plants. One estimate puts the plant–opisthokont split at the last common ancestor of living eukaryotes, about 1.0–1.3 billion years ago (Chernikova et al. 2011). Other estimates run older.
- **No cross is known.** The only genetic exchange found between plants and fungi is horizontal transfer of single genes. It is "exceedingly rare, particularly among the angiosperms", a few cases over their whole history (Richards et al. 2009).

**Among close relatives, isolation grows with divergence.**
- **In yeasts it rises smoothly.** In *Saccharomyces*, hybrid sterility rises with sequence divergence in "a smooth monotonic relationship", with no sharp step from fertile to sterile (Liti, Barton & Louis 2006). The cause is the cell's mismatch repair acting on divergent sequences, not a few major genes.
- **In mushroom-forming fungi it tracks divergence too.** Among allopatric species of Homobasidiomycota, isolation correlates with genetic divergence. Sympatric sibling species have stronger premating isolation than allopatric ones, the pattern expected from reinforcement. In Ascomycota neither pattern holds (Le Gac & Giraud 2008).
- **The same holds in animals.** In *Drosophila*, isolation rises gradually with genetic distance (Coyne & Orr 1989, 1997).

**Whether trait distance stands in for isolation.** The domain ties isolation to ancestry: time and sequence divergence, not shared traits. Isolation is graded among close relatives and complete long before the distance between a fungus and a plant. A distance in trait space follows isolation only where traits diverge with ancestry. Where unrelated lineages converge on a similar way of life, the domain shows no gene flow. A lichen fungus builds a photosynthesising body with an alga, and it does not cross with plants. That trait distance is a fair stand-in only within a clade is an inference from these sources, not a finding of any of them.

**No fungus photosynthesises. It reaches photosynthate only through a partner.**
- **Eukaryotes gained photosynthesis by taking in a photosynthetic cell, and rarely.** Primary plastids arose twice in the history of eukaryotes: in the ancestor of plants and algae about 1.6 billion years ago, and in the amoeba *Paulinella* 90–140 million years ago (Gabr, Grossman & Bhattacharya 2020).
- **Lichenisation is the fungal route, and it has arisen often.** It was first shown to have arisen at least five times (Gargas et al. 1995). The 2016 classification counts 20–30 independent origins: 14–23 in Ascomycota and 6–7 in Basidiomycota (Lücking, Hodkinson & Leavitt 2016).
- **It is lost more often than gained.** In Ascomycota, losses of lichenisation outnumber gains, and in 90.6 % of sampled trees the loss rate exceeds the gain rate (Lutzoni, Pagel & Reeb 2001). Some lost lineages became decomposers or plant parasites.
- **It is slow.** Lichens arose after vascular plants (Nelsen et al. 2020). The main lineages of lichen-forming Ascomycota go back at least to the Carboniferous (Prieto & Wedin 2013). 20–30 origins over a few hundred million years is roughly one origin per ten million years across all fungi. That rate is arithmetic on these sources, not a figure from them.
- **A lichenised fungus is still a heterotroph.** The photobiont exports fixed carbon to the fungus. The fungus makes up most of the lichen's biomass and appears to regulate the size of the photobiont population (Palmqvist 2000). So lichenisation makes a fungus a consumer of a living, photosynthesising partner. It does not make the fungus an autotroph.
- **The partnership can form at once; the lineage does not change.** A yeast and the alga *Chlamydomonas* formed an obligate mutualism as soon as their environment required them to swap carbon and nitrogen. The capacity extended to other fungi and other *Chlamydomonas* species (Hom & Murray 2014). This is an ecological pairing of two lineages that stay distinct, each in its own mode.
- **Heterotrophic fungi do switch among heterotrophic modes.** The ectomycorrhizal habit is found in 82–86 separate fungal lineages (Tedersoo & Smith 2017). That is movement among ways of being a heterotroph, not toward autotrophy.

**At a stand's scale, constraint holds the lineage, not selection.** Over a few hundred generations, the domain gives a saprotroph no route to autotrophy. It has no photosynthetic machinery, no cross with plants, and lichenisation takes millions of years and still leaves it a heterotroph. Whether selection on the litter would also hold it there is not tested by any source found. On the domain's evidence, constraint alone is enough.

*Evidence quality.*
- **Read in part from the full text:** Chernikova et al. (for the divergence date).
- **Read from abstracts and summaries:** Richards et al., Liti et al., Le Gac & Giraud, Gabr et al., Gargas et al., Lücking et al., Lutzoni et al., Nelsen et al., Prieto & Wedin, Palmqvist, Hom & Murray and Tedersoo & Smith. The count of lichen origins comes from a summary of Lücking et al.'s abstract. The 90.6 % comes from a summary of Lutzoni et al. That some lineages that lost lichenisation became decomposers or parasites comes from press coverage of Nelsen et al.'s 2020 *PNAS* study, not the paper.
- **Recalled, not read:** Coyne & Orr (1989, 1997), cited for the general pattern only.
- **Not verified:** Palmqvist's and Prieto & Wedin's authorship and details, and Lutzoni et al.'s co-authors and pages, all recalled; the full title of Tedersoo & Smith's chapter; the issue and page numbers of Liti et al., Gargas et al. and Coyne & Orr.
- **What the domain gives:** *categorical* isolation between fungi and plants; *graded* isolation among close relatives, tied to ancestry; autotrophy reached by fungi only through a partner, on a timescale of millions of years, and leaving the fungus a heterotroph.
- **What it leaves open:** a test of trait distance against isolation across distant lineages, and any measure of selection holding a saprotroph at the heterotroph end within a stand.
