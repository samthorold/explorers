# Fungi

Fungi occupy a unique position in ecosystem architecture: they are the primary agents responsible for closing nutrient loops that would otherwise break open. Where producers fix energy and consumers transfer it up trophic levels, fungi operate across the system's seams — decomposing locked organic matter back into bioavailable nutrients, forming resource-sharing infrastructure between producers, and regulating population dynamics through parasitism. From a systems dynamics perspective, fungi are less a trophic level and more a set of coupling mechanisms that connect stocks and regulate flows throughout the system.

## Systemic role

In systems ecology terms, fungi serve three distinct systemic functions:

1. **Nutrient liberators (saprotrophic).** Dead organic matter represents a stock of locked energy and nutrients — carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, sulfur. Without enzymatic decomposition, this stock grows monotonically. Saprotrophic fungi are the primary agents capable of breaking down recalcitrant compounds like lignin and cellulose, converting locked stocks into bioavailable flows. They are the rate-limiting step in the detrital pathway of energy flow (Moore et al. 2004).

2. **Resource-redistribution infrastructure (mycorrhizal).** Mycorrhizal fungi form networks connecting the root systems of multiple plants, creating a transport layer for carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and water. This transforms the nutrient landscape from a collection of isolated agents competing for local soil resources into a partially connected system where resources can flow between producers based on supply and demand gradients. The network topology exhibits scale-free and small-world properties (Simard 2018), meaning a few highly-connected hub nodes (large established trees) disproportionately influence system-wide resource distribution.

3. **Population regulators (parasitic).** Fungal pathogens create density-dependent mortality, acting as a negative feedback on host populations. This prevents any single species from monopolising resources indefinitely and maintains diversity through mechanisms analogous to Janzen-Connell effects — high-density populations of a single species become increasingly vulnerable to specialist pathogens.

These three roles map onto a single systemic principle: fungi prevent the accumulation of any stock to the point where it destabilises the system — whether that stock is dead organic matter, spatially concentrated nutrients, or a monoculture population.

## Energy and nutrient flows

Fungi participate in two of the major energy pathways identified in ecosystem ecology:

**The detrital pathway.** Lindeman (1942) established that ecosystems have two primary energy channels: the grazer pathway (energy flows from living producers to consumers) and the detrital pathway (energy flows from dead organic matter through decomposers). In most terrestrial ecosystems, the detrital pathway processes the majority of net primary production — often 80-90% of plant biomass enters the dead organic matter pool rather than being consumed live.

Fungi are the dominant decomposers of plant-derived organic matter because they produce extracellular enzymes (lignin peroxidases, manganese peroxidases, laccases) capable of breaking down lignin, the structural polymer that makes up 15-40% of plant dry mass. Bacteria cannot efficiently decompose lignin. This gives fungi a near-monopoly on the rate-limiting step of terrestrial decomposition.

Key flow parameters:
- **Substrate quality controls decomposition rate.** The lignin:nitrogen ratio of organic matter is the best single predictor of decomposition rate. High lignin:N material (wood) decomposes slowly; low lignin:N material (leaf litter) decomposes quickly.
- **Carbon use efficiency.** Fungi convert roughly 40-55% of consumed carbon into biomass, with the remainder respired as CO2. This is substantially higher than bacterial carbon use efficiency (~20-30%), meaning fungi sequester more carbon per unit consumed into the microbial biomass stock.
- **Nutrient stoichiometry.** Fungal biomass has an average C:N ratio of ~11:1 (11.2 ± 6.4 across 271 samples; Pánek et al. 2024), but wood-decay fungi can reach C:N ratios above 40:1. Fungi must acquire nitrogen from their substrate or soil environment, creating a coupling between decomposition rate and nitrogen availability.

**The mycorrhizal pathway.** Mycorrhizal fungi create a bidirectional flow: they receive 10-30% of a host plant's photosynthetically fixed carbon, and in return supply the plant with soil-derived phosphorus, nitrogen, and water. This is not charity — it is a market-like exchange governed by local supply-demand ratios (Kiers et al. 2011, "Reciprocal rewards stabilize cooperation in the mycorrhizal symbiosis"). Plants allocate more carbon to fungal partners that deliver more phosphorus, and fungi allocate more phosphorus to plant partners that deliver more carbon.

At the system level, this creates an energy flow that bypasses the detrital pathway entirely: carbon fixed by producers flows directly into the soil fungal network, fueling nutrient acquisition that feeds back into producer growth.

## Feedback loops

Fungi participate in several reinforcing and balancing feedback loops:

**Reinforcing: Decomposition-growth loop.**
Dead organic matter --> fungal decomposition --> nutrient release to soil --> producer uptake and growth --> more organic matter production --> more dead organic matter. This is the fundamental nutrient cycling loop of terrestrial ecosystems. Fungi set the speed of this loop through their decomposition rate.

**Reinforcing: Mycorrhizal mutualism loop.**
Healthy producers --> more carbon allocation to mycorrhizal fungi --> larger fungal network --> more nutrient acquisition --> healthier producers. This positive feedback amplifies producer productivity in nutrient-poor environments and is a major driver of forest establishment and succession.

**Balancing: Nutrient limitation on decomposition.**
Rapid decomposition --> nutrient release --> producer growth --> increased lignin production (as plants grow larger and woodier) --> slower decomposition of high-lignin material. As ecosystems mature, the average substrate quality declines, slowing the decomposition loop. This is a key mechanism behind carbon accumulation in old-growth forests.

**Balancing: Parasitic density-dependence.**
High host density --> increased pathogen transmission --> host mortality --> reduced host density --> reduced pathogen transmission. Fungal pathogens create classic boom-bust dynamics (Burdon & Laine 2019) that prevent competitive dominance and maintain species diversity. This is a stabilising force at the community level.

**Balancing: Mycorrhizal resource redistribution.**
Shade/stress on some producers --> reduced photosynthesis --> those producers receive more nutrients via mycorrhizal network from hub trees --> partial recovery. The mycorrhizal network acts as a buffer against local resource shocks, damping oscillations in producer populations. The extent and significance of this redistribution is actively debated in the literature (Karst et al. 2023).

## Key strategies (saprotrophic, mycorrhizal, parasitic — from a flows perspective)

These are not taxonomic categories but functional strategies defined by their position in the system's flow architecture:

### Saprotrophic strategy

**Flow position:** Dead organic matter stock --> fungal biomass + bioavailable nutrient flow to soil.

Saprotrophic fungi are the system's recyclers. They consume a stock (dead organic matter) that no other functional group can efficiently process. Their decomposition rate determines how fast the locked nutrient stock turns over.

Key system-level distinctions among saprotrophs:
- **White-rot fungi** decompose both lignin and cellulose, leaving a bleached residue. They are the only organisms capable of fully mineralising lignin. Without them, woody material accumulates — the Carboniferous period (360-300 Mya) saw massive coal formation in part because lignin-degrading Agaricomycetes had not yet evolved their full enzymatic arsenal (Floudas et al. 2012, Science). Once white-rot fungi appeared (~290 Mya), the rate of coal formation dropped sharply, though the relative contribution of fungal evolution vs. climate and tectonics remains debated (Nelsen et al. 2016, PNAS).
- **Brown-rot fungi** decompose cellulose but leave lignin largely intact, producing a brown, crumbly residue that contributes to stable soil organic matter (humus). They create a flow from labile carbon to the atmosphere while routing recalcitrant carbon into long-term soil stocks.
- **Soft-rot fungi** decompose cellulose in high-moisture, low-oxygen environments where white and brown-rot fungi are less competitive — a niche-specific decomposition pathway.

**How a saprotroph reaches its litter: by growth, not travel.** A saprotrophic fungus does not move to its food. It grows into the litter and through the substrate between pieces of it.
- **Resource-unit restricted or not.** Some saprotrophs stay inside the piece of litter they colonised and spread between pieces only by spores. Cord-forming basidiomycetes are *non-resource-unit restricted*: they grow out of one resource in search of others, and their cord systems join many pieces of litter into one long-lived network at the soil–litter interface. These systems span several square metres up to hectares and persist for years (Boddy 1999).
- **Sit-and-wait and seek-and-find.** A cord system can lie as a standing network on which new litter lands ("sit and wait"), or grow out to find it ("seek and find"). Most do both at once (Boddy 1999).
- **Extent grows with biomass, sublinearly.** A two-dimensional mycelium's biomass scales with its radius as `M ∝ R^D`, where `D` is the mass fractal dimension (Bolton & Boddy 1993). Slow, diffuse search fronts that fill space have `D` close to 2. Open systems of fast-extending cords have lower `D`. *Phanerochaete velutina* systems measure about 1.7 (Bretherton et al. 2006). So reach grows as `M^(1/D)`, between `M^0.5` for a filled disc and about `M^0.6` for a cord system. A larger mycelium touches more ground, but less than in proportion to its biomass.
- **Extent follows the food.** Radial extension and biomass production respond to the size and quality of the resources a system meets (Donnelly & Boddy 1997). Mycelium on ground with no new resource regresses, and its biomass is redeployed (see *How decomposers find food* in [spatial ecology](spatial-ecology.md)).

- **Reach does not decide diet; defence does.** The largest known mycelium, an *Armillaria ostoyae* clone in Oregon's Blue Mountains, covers about 965 ha. Its rhizomorphs carry food across poor ground between stumps, and where one meets a susceptible conifer it enters under the bark, kills the cambium and feeds on the wood. So it is a pathogen as well as a saprotroph. Most large cord-forming saprotrophs, such as *Phanerochaete* and *Hypholoma*, live on dead wood and litter, though their networks span metres. What keeps a saprotroph off living plants is the plant's immunity against colonisation of living tissue, which death removes and which a necrotroph's effectors and toxins overcome. The size of the network does not decide it ([plants](plants.md), *Defence against microbes*).

*Evidence quality.* The cord-system picture rests on one research group's microcosm and field work, read here from abstracts, reviews and reference lists, not from the papers' full texts. The fractal measurements come from two-dimensional microcosms. The domain gives the **form** of the scaling: sublinear, with an exponent between 0.5 and 0.6. It gives the **magnitude** only as an order: a non-unit-restricted saprotroph's network spans many pieces of litter, and a unit-restricted one spans one. It gives no figure for reach per unit of biomass that would carry over to another world's units. The *Armillaria* figures come from encyclopedic and popular summaries, whose mass estimates conflict. That defence, not reach, separates a saprotroph from a pathogen is now sourced in [plants](plants.md), *Defence against microbes*.

### How much litter one saprotroph commands, and what it costs to keep

**Catchment.** A non-unit-restricted saprotroph's genet commands litter from many plants, and many genets share each plant's litter.
- **Large genets.**
  - Cord systems of *Phanerochaete velutina* in woodland are "commonly over 20 m" long (Thompson 1982).
  - Isolates of one *Tricholomopsis platyphylla* genet were found up to 150 m apart in a 20.8 ha wood, with other genets between them (Thompson & Rayner 1982).
  - Cord systems join litter over "many square metres or even hectares" and persist for years (Boddy et al. 2009).
  - Fairy-ring litter decomposers advance 7–75 cm a year. A *Clitocybe geotropa* ring 800 m across is estimated at about 700 years old (Zotti et al. 2025).
- **Small genets are common too.** Litter genets of *Marasmius rotula*, *Mycetinis alliaceus* and *Gymnopus androsaceus* with two or more sporocarps span 0.3–4 m, and one reaches about 15 m (Bošković et al. 2017, 2019).
- **Unit-restricted saprotrophs and bacteria** command less than one plant's litter.
- **No source counts plants per genet.** The literature gives genet spans, not catchments in plant individuals.

**Decomposer biomass against its supply.**
- **Global stocks.** Fungi hold about 12 Gt C (uncertain threefold) against about 450 Gt C of plants: about 2.7 % of plant biomass, or about 8 % of non-woody plant biomass (Bar-On et al. 2018).
- **Soil microbes.** Soil microbial biomass is 1.2 % of soil organic C, about 23 Pg C to 1 m depth (Xu et al. 2013). That is about half a year of global soil heterotrophic respiration, about 50 Pg C a year (Hashimoto et al. 2015).
- **In litter.** Fungal biomass is about 0.07–0.6 % of forest litter's dry mass, from biomarkers calibrated on the dominant basidiomycetes' fruit bodies (Baldrian et al. 2013). Part of that tenfold range is disagreement between biomarkers, not between stands.
  - **It is a stand-level stock, not a catchment.** A biomarker extracted from a bulk sample measures every fungus in it at once, and one gram of litter holds many taxa that turn over within months (Voříšková & Baldrian 2013). The ratio is the whole decomposer community's standing biomass against the litter it stands in. It says nothing about how much litter one individual reaches.
  - **Fungi are not all of it.** They are a mean 35–76 % of microbial biomass, litter layers included (Joergensen & Wichern 2008), so all the microbes stand at roughly 1.3–3 times the fungal figure.
  - **Other litters carry more.** On oak litter the fungal stock peaks near 5 % and settles near 2.6 % (Šnajdr et al. 2011, below). On leaves decomposing in streams it peaks at 10–13 % (Suberkropp 2001). Baldrian's range is the low end of what decomposing litter carries.
- **The ratio rises as litter is colonised.** In oak litterbags, ergosterol goes from 3.2 µg/g in green leaves to 24 in fresh litter, peaks at 202 at month 2, and holds near 100 from month 4 to 24 (Šnajdr et al. 2011). At Baldrian's conversion that is about 0.08 %, 0.6 %, 5 % and 2.6 % of the litter's mass. The rise is mostly one opportunist, *Mycosphaerella*, multiplying sevenfold (Voříšková & Baldrian 2013).
- **The founders are already in the leaf, and small.** Phyllosphere fungi, endophytes and surface fungi from 41 genera, are 2–100 % of the early decomposer community and mostly vanish early in decay (Osono 2006). So a stand's founding decomposers are a small fraction of the settled stock and grow into the litter. Later arrivals take litter by primary capture of uncolonised resource, then by combat for colonised resource, ending in deadlock or replacement (Boddy 2000). No source gives a founder count per unit of litter.
- **Turnover.** Fungal biomass in soil turns over in 130–150 days (Rousk & Bååth 2007). Litter has a half-life of 0.5–4 years ([nutrient cycling](nutrient-cycling.md), *Decomposition kinetics*). So a decomposer lives many of its own generations on one cohort of litter.

**Maintenance.**
- **Plant tissue** spends about 1–4 % of its dry mass a day on maintenance. That is "low compared to animals and micro-organisms" (Penning de Vries 1974).
- **Fungi in pure culture** spend 12–70 % a day (Carter et al. 1971).
- **Active soil communities in situ** spend about 1–2.2 % of microbial C a day, counting turnover with maintenance (Schimel & Weintraub 2003).
- **So per unit of living, active tissue,** a saprotroph pays the same order as plant tissue, or more.
- **Per unit of standing biomass,** both pay far less, for different reasons.
  - About 70 % of plant biomass is metabolically inert wood (Bar-On et al. 2018).
  - Most decomposer biomass is dormant or empty:
    - the active share of soil microbial biomass is about 4–49 %, typically about 20 % (compiled by Wang et al. 2014);
    - in one pine-forest soil only 2–4 % of fungal biomass was active (Söderström 1979);
    - dormant biomass pays two to three orders of magnitude less maintenance than active biomass (Anderson & Domsch 1985).

**Throughput.**
- *Gymnopus androsaceus*, alone on pine needles at 20 °C, decomposed about 5 g of litter C for each g of mycelial C it built over 10 months, roughly 1–2 % a day (Boberg et al. 2014).
- The domain gives no general rate per unit of decomposer biomass. In the field, fungal biomass predicts decay rate poorly.

**Pooling.** A basidiomycete mycelium translocates carbohydrate and nutrient through the whole network, so it works as one physiological unit (Lindahl & Olsson 2004).
- **Rich resources feed the search across poor ground.** Connecting old litter to fresh moves nitrogen and growth into the fresh litter, and raises the fungus's carbon-use efficiency (Boberg et al. 2014).
- **How far a mycelium reaches.** In Davidson and Olsson's model, a mycelium growing from a food base reaches a maximum distance. That distance is set by the size of the food base against the maintenance of the connecting mycelium (via Lindahl & Olsson 2004).

*Evidence quality.* Read in full text: Bar-On et al., Xu et al., Penning de Vries, Schimel & Weintraub, Boberg et al., Lindahl & Olsson, Wang et al., the Zotti et al. review, Šnajdr et al. and Voříšková & Baldrian.
- **Read from abstracts:** Thompson, Thompson & Rayner, the Bošković studies, Rousk & Bååth, Söderström, Anderson & Domsch, Hashimoto et al., Baldrian et al. (its methods were not read), Joergensen & Wichern, Suberkropp, Osono and Boddy 2000.
- **Converted here, not by the sources:** the oak series' percentages, from Šnajdr's ergosterol at Baldrian's 3.8 mg per g of fungal biomass.
- **From a secondary summary:** Boddy et al. 2009.
- **Taken from the review, not read:** the primary fairy-ring studies behind Zotti et al.
- **Not verified:** some titles and journal details for Thompson & Rayner and the Bošković studies.
- **What the domain gives:** a *form* for catchment (one genet spans many plants' litter, and reach is limited by food base against the maintenance of the connecting mycelium); *orders* for the stand's decomposer biomass against its litter, and how it rises from founding to settled, and for active-tissue maintenance.
- **What it leaves open:** a reach per unit of biomass that carries to another world's units, the litter one individual commands, the number of founders per unit of litter, the living fraction of a field cord system, and a general decomposition rate per unit of decomposer biomass.

### The saprotroph's life history: ageing, upkeep, and when and how it reproduces

**Ageing.** A saprotroph's mycelium is modular and grows without a set end. The genet as a whole is not expected to age, and most do not.
- **The theory.** A fungus has no separate germ line. It grows radially, so its size, and roughly its fecundity, rise with age. Selection then opposes senescence, as Hamilton (1966, via Maas) argued for organisms that keep growing. "Systemic senescence is expected to be absent or greatly delayed in fungi" (Maas 2005).
- **Long-lived genets.**
  - One *Armillaria gallica* genet covers about 75 ha of forest floor and is at least 2,500 years old, from measured rhizomorph growth rates. Its genome barely changed across samples more than 100 m apart (Anderson et al. 2018).
  - A *Clitocybe geotropa* fairy ring is estimated at about 700 years old (Zotti et al. 2025; *Catchment*, above).
- **The exceptions live on short-lived substrates.** Every natural isolate of the dung fungus *Podospora anserina* ages and dies within weeks (Rizet 1953, via Maas 2005), two to three weeks in culture (van Diepeningen et al. 2014). Senescence is also reported in other dung fungi (*Podospora curvicolla*, *Ascobolus*, *Sordaria*) and in some *Neurospora* isolates. In those *Neurospora* isolates it is caused by a mitochondrial plasmid (Bertrand, Chan & Griffiths 1985). Maas (2005) reads the pattern ecologically. A fungus on a substrate that is limited in both space and time, and that reproduces early and copiously, can evolve to age. One that is not so limited, like *Armillaria* in soil, is expected to live very long. Fungal senescence is "almost invariably" an accumulation of defective mitochondrial DNA, not a shortening of telomeres.
- **What age means at each scale.**
  - *The genet* is everything grown from one mating. Its age is the time since then, and it has no age-tied hazard in most species.
  - *Hyphae and compartments* turn over. Fungal biomass in soil turns over in 130–150 days (Rousk & Bååth 2007). Old compartments are emptied and their contents moved to the growing front (*Upkeep as it grows*, below).
  - *Fruit bodies* are short-lived modules. In *Coprinopsis cinerea* the cap autolyses once its spores are shed (Kües 2000).
- **What kills a genet is ecological.** Its resource runs out. A rival replaces it in combat (Boddy 2000). It is grazed. "Individuals of *Armillaria* cannot exist in stasis over the long term" (Anderson et al. 2018). The authors suggest, untested, that most genets die soon after they start.
- **So a resource-unit-restricted genet lives about as long as its piece of litter.** This is an inference from the above, not a measurement. Leaf litter has a half-life of 0.5–4 years ([nutrient cycling](nutrient-cycling.md), *Decomposition kinetics*), and a leaf's fungal community turns over within months (Voříšková & Baldrian 2013). A cord-former that moves between pieces is not bounded this way.

**Upkeep as it grows.** A mycelium's upkeep tracks its living, active part, not its whole biomass. As a mycelium grows into its litter, the share that is old, emptied or abandoned rises.
- **Old mycelium is emptied and its contents moved forward.** Cytoplasm in the mature region is broken down by autophagy and carried to the tips (Shoji, Arioka & Kitamoto 2006). Cord systems withdraw from unproductive regions and redeploy that biomass (Harris & Boddy 2005). Both are covered in [energy flow](energy-flow.md), *Lean times*.
- **Older mycelium respires less.** Whole-culture respiration of *Rhizoctonia solani* and *Sclerotium bataticola* fell with age (Van Etten, Molitoris & Gottlieb 1966). In *Cryphonectria parasitica* colonies "the metabolic rate steadily declines with age and differentiation of the mycelium" (Dawe et al. 2009). That study measured CO₂ per unit of colony area, not per unit of biomass.
- **In decomposing litter, most of the mycelium ends up dead.** In needle litter of three conifer forests, respiration fell with litter age, and "in far-decomposed litter only a minor fraction of the total mycelium is alive" (Virzo De Santo et al. 2002).
- **A mycelium abandons an exhausted food base.** *Phanerochaete velutina* grew from a wood block to a new one. The original block was then moved to fresh soil, and only 67 % of them regrew. Whether the mycelium left depended on the new resource reaching a threshold size (Fukasawa, Savoury & Boddy 2020).
- **The order of the saving.** Only 2–4 % of fungal biomass in a pine-forest soil was active. Dormant biomass pays two to three orders of magnitude less maintenance than active biomass (*Maintenance*, above).
- **The domain gives no exponent.** No source found gives how a mycelium's maintenance scales with its size, nor the living fraction of a field genet as it ages. The form is clear: the cost of keeping a mycelium grows more slowly than the mycelium, because old parts are emptied, recycled or left behind.

**Provisioning reproduction.** A mycelium provisions its fruit bodies from stores and from material moved through the network, not only from what it is drawing at the time.
- **Stored carbon moves into the fruit body.** In *Coprinopsis cinerea*, "degradation of glycogen and translocation from the mycelium to the developing fruiting-bodies have been demonstrated" early in fruiting. Young fruit bodies draw a flow of nutrients toward themselves. Transport through the vegetative mycelium is slow, so early fruiting likely relies on carbon "stored in place" (Kües 2000).
- **The network redirects to the fruit bodies.** In *Agaricus bisporus*, a tracer amino acid moves toward the colony edge while the mycelium grows. When mushrooms start to form, its flow switches to them, through the mycelium and its cords (Herman et al. 2020).
- **Spores can be built from existing mycelium.** Aquatic hyphomycetes on leaves in streams put about half of their new production into conidia. Without added nitrogen and phosphorus, their ergosterol fell while sporulation "continued at the initial level". That points to existing mycelium being turned into spores (Sridhar & Bärlocher 2000).
- **Fruit bodies are rich in nitrogen and phosphorus.** Across 214 species, fruit bodies of litter and soil saprotrophs average 6.4 % N by dry mass, at C:N about 7. Those of wood saprotrophs average 3.5 % N, at C:N about 16 (Pánek et al. 2024). Plant tissue runs at C:N 20–80 ([nutrient cycling](nutrient-cycling.md), *Stoichiometric constraints*). So a litter saprotroph's fruit body holds nitrogen several times more concentrated than the litter it grew on. That much is arithmetic, not a finding of these sources.
- **Which element limits fruiting is open.** In *Coprinopsis*, more carbon or more nitrogen in the medium each act against fruiting, and fruiting is "particularly sensitive to balanced C/N ratios" (Kües 2000). No field study found shows nitrogen or phosphorus as the element that limits a litter saprotroph's fruiting.

**When it reproduces.** Two patterns run side by side. Mushroom-forming fungi fruit when their nutrients run out, given the right weather. Leaf-litter microfungi sporulate while they grow.
- **Depletion triggers fruiting.** *Coprinopsis cinerea* "initiates fruiting when nutrients are depleted", but "only the youngest mycelium maintains fruiting competence". Glucose inhibits fruiting (Kües 2000). The full set of triggers is light, temperature, humidity and nutrients. Starvation alone is not enough. [Energy flow](energy-flow.md), *Lean times*, has more examples: *Bacillus* sporulation, myxobacterial fruiting, and spores built by autophagy in *Aspergillus*.
- **Theory puts the switch before exhaustion.** In an optimal-allocation model of a saprotroph on one patch, the fungus grows first, then switches all at once to spores. It switches before the patch is used up (Gilchrist, Sulsky & Pringle 2006).
- **Sporulation alongside growth.** Aquatic hyphomycetes on stream leaves put about half of their production into conidia from early in decay. In one stream, ergosterol and spore production peaked together, at five weeks (Sridhar & Bärlocher 2000, data not shown in the paper).
- **What is open.** No field study found follows one litter basidiomycete's genet on a known cohort of litter, to say whether it fruits as that litter is exhausted or while it is still growing into it.

*Evidence quality.*
- **Read in full text:** Maas's thesis introduction.
- **Read in part from the full text:** Kües (2000), about two-fifths of it; Anderson et al. (2018); Sridhar & Bärlocher; Dawe et al.; and Pánek et al. (its Table 1).
- **Read from abstracts and summaries:** van Diepeningen et al., Virzo De Santo et al., Fukasawa et al., Herman et al., Gilchrist et al. and Van Etten et al. Shoji et al. and Harris & Boddy are as read for [energy flow](energy-flow.md).
- **From a secondary summary:** Bertrand et al.
- **Not verified:** the pages of Van Etten et al., Bertrand et al. and Herman et al., and the title of Virzo De Santo et al.
- **Taken from Maas, not read:** Rizet (1953), Hamilton (1966), and the other dung-fungus cases.
- **What the domain gives:** a *form* for ageing (a modular genet with no general age-tied hazard, and senescence in a minority on short-lived substrates), and a *form* for upkeep (cost tracks the living part, which falls as a share of the mycelium). For reproduction it gives *mechanisms*: translocation from stores, spores built from existing mycelium, depletion as one trigger among several, and sporulation alongside growth in leaf microfungi. One *magnitude* carries: about half of an aquatic hyphomycete's production goes to spores.
- **What it leaves open:** an exponent for upkeep against size, the age at which field genets die, the share of a terrestrial litter saprotroph's production that goes to fruit bodies, and the element that limits fruiting in the field.

How a saprotroph is kept from crossing with plants, and what holds its lineage at the heterotroph end, are in [trophic roles](trophic-roles.md), *A decomposer lineage stays a decomposer*.

### Mycorrhizal strategy

**Flow position:** Producer carbon stock --> fungal biomass; soil nutrient stock --> producer nutrient uptake (mediated by fungi).

Mycorrhizal fungi are the system's connective tissue. They create flows between stocks that would otherwise be uncoupled:

- **Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF)** associate with ~80% of land plant species. They primarily enhance phosphorus uptake. The network is relatively non-specific — many plant species connect through the same fungal network, creating broad resource-sharing potential.
- **Ectomycorrhizal fungi (ECM)** associate primarily with trees in temperate and boreal forests. They form dense hyphal mantles around root tips and can decompose soil organic matter to access organically bound nitrogen (Lindahl et al. 2007). This dual role — mutualist and decomposer — means ECM fungi create a direct coupling between the carbon-for-nutrients exchange and the decomposition loop.
- **Ericoid mycorrhizal fungi** associate with heathland plants (Ericaceae) on acidic, organic, nutrient-poor soils. Like ECM fungi, they produce enzymes that release nitrogen and phosphorus from organic matter and pass them to the host (Read & Perez-Moreno 2003; Smith & Read 2008).

**The producer's route to organic nutrient.** Plants take up nutrient mainly in mineral form (ammonium, nitrate, phosphate). They do not digest dead matter themselves (see [trophic roles](trophic-roles.md), *Why decomposition falls to specialists*). Where most of the soil's nitrogen and phosphorus is held in organic matter, a plant reaches it through ECM or ericoid partners, which break that matter down and hand the nutrient over in exchange for carbon. Read & Perez-Moreno (2003) describe a gradient across ecosystems. Arbuscular mycorrhizal plants dominate where nutrient cycles mainly in mineral form. ECM and ericoid plants dominate where it is held in organic matter: boreal forest and heathland. So a producer short of nutrient pays a decomposer-capable partner to do the decomposing. It does not become a decomposer.

From a network perspective, the mycorrhizal strategy transforms point-to-point resource competition into a network flow problem. The implications for system dynamics are significant: connected plant communities show reduced variance in individual fitness, greater resilience to local perturbations, and altered competitive dynamics (van der Heijden et al. 1998).

### Parasitic strategy

**Flow position:** Living host energy stock --> fungal biomass (at host's expense).

Parasitic fungi extract energy from living organisms, creating a direct drain on host fitness. System-level effects:

- **Density-dependent regulation.** Transmission increases with host density, creating negative feedback that caps population growth. This is functionally equivalent to a predator in the grazer pathway but operates within the producer trophic level.
- **Diversity maintenance.** Specialist pathogens disproportionately impact dominant species (the species with the highest local density), creating openings for competitors. This is a key mechanism behind the high diversity of tropical forests (Bagchi et al. 2014).
- **Boom-bust dynamics.** Host-pathogen systems frequently exhibit oscillatory dynamics — host populations grow until pathogen pressure builds, then crash, allowing recovery when pathogen populations decline due to reduced host availability.

## ABM and computational literature

Agent-based and individual-based models of fungal systems are relatively sparse compared to, say, predator-prey or vegetation dynamics models, but several lines of work are directly relevant:

### Hyphal growth and mycelial network models

**Falconer et al. (2005, 2007, 2012).** Ruth Falconer and colleagues developed individual-based models of fungal colony growth where individual hyphal tips are the agents. These models incorporate nutrient absorption, intramycelial transport, biomass recycling, and branching decisions. Key insight: colony-level behaviour (radial growth rate, density patterns, resource allocation) emerges from simple hyphal-tip rules interacting with local nutrient fields. Published in *Fungal Genetics and Biology* and *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*. The work connects to the Grimm/Railsback tradition of pattern-oriented modeling — colony-level patterns are used to validate individual-level rules.

**Boswell et al. (2007).** Developed a hybrid model combining a continuous nutrient field with discrete hyphal agents, showing how nutrient translocation within networks leads to non-trivial foraging patterns. The model demonstrates that fungal networks can exhibit adaptive resource allocation without centralised control — a key property for ABM implementations.

### Mycorrhizal network models

**Simard and colleagues.** While Simard's primary work is empirical, it has generated modeling work on mycorrhizal networks as graphs. The network topology (scale-free, small-world) has been characterised and modeled using network science approaches. The key modeling implication: a few hub nodes (mother trees) disproportionately control resource flows, and their removal causes cascading effects on seedling survival.

**Fellbaum et al. (2014) / Kiers et al. (2011).** These studies modeled the mycorrhizal symbiosis as a biological market, where resource exchange ratios between plants and fungi are governed by local supply-demand dynamics. This market framing translates directly into ABM architectures: agents (plants, fungi) exchange resources based on local prices, and system-level allocation patterns emerge.

### Decomposition and nutrient cycling models

**Moore et al. (2005) — Detritus, trophic dynamics, and biodiversity.** While not strictly an ABM, this work models the detrital food web as a system of interacting functional groups, showing how energy flow through the decomposer pathway (dominated by fungi and bacteria) interacts with the grazer pathway to determine community structure and stability. Provides a systems-dynamics framework that could be implemented as an ABM.

**Moore et al. (2015) — Decomposition by ectomycorrhizal fungi alters soil carbon storage.** A simulation model showing that when ECM fungi decompose soil organic matter (the "Gadgil effect"), it alters the balance between carbon inputs and outputs in forest soils. Demonstrates the importance of the dual decomposer-mutualist role of ECM fungi for system-level carbon dynamics.

**CENTURY and RothC models.** These are not ABMs but compartment models of soil carbon turnover that represent fungal vs. bacterial decomposition pathways with different turnover rates. They provide baseline parameters (decomposition rate constants, carbon use efficiencies) useful for calibrating fungal agents in ABMs.

### Network and graph-theoretic approaches

**Bebber et al. (2007).** Characterised the network architecture of fungal mycelia, showing that they form efficient transport networks with properties similar to engineered distribution systems. This provides a quantitative basis for modeling nutrient transport through fungal networks in ABMs.

**Heaton et al. (2012).** Modeled fungal networks as adaptive transport systems, showing that mycelia can solve optimisation problems (connecting food sources efficiently) through local growth rules. Relevant for ABM implementations where fungal agents must allocate resources across a spatial network.

### Gaps in the ABM literature

The most notable gap is the lack of integrated models that combine all three fungal strategies (saprotrophic, mycorrhizal, parasitic) within a single ecosystem ABM. Most models focus on one strategy in isolation. The key unresolved modeling challenge is representing:
1. The decomposition loop (dead matter --> bioavailable nutrients) with realistic rate-limiting by substrate quality.
2. The mycorrhizal network as a transport/redistribution layer connecting producer agents.
3. Parasitic fungi as density-dependent population regulators.

## System without fungi

The thought experiment of removing fungi reveals their systemic importance through the failures that would cascade:

**Immediate: Decomposition bottleneck.** Without saprotrophic fungi, lignin-rich organic matter (wood, tough leaf litter) stops decomposing efficiently. Bacteria can handle labile compounds (sugars, simple proteins) but cannot break down lignin. Dead organic matter accumulates. The locked nutrient stock grows while the bioavailable nutrient flow to soil collapses. This is not hypothetical — the Carboniferous period provides a geological-scale natural experiment: before fungi evolved efficient lignin decomposition, dead plant material accumulated as peat at rates sufficient to form the world's major coal deposits (Floudas et al. 2012, though see Nelsen et al. 2016 for caveats).

**Short-term: Producer nutrient starvation.** As nutrient cycling slows, soil nutrient stocks deplete. Producers can no longer access sufficient nitrogen and phosphorus for growth. Net primary productivity declines. This is compounded by the loss of mycorrhizal networks, which in many ecosystems are responsible for 80-90% of plant phosphorus uptake (van der Heijden et al. 1998). Plants that depend on mycorrhizal phosphorus acquisition — which is most of them — would face severe nutrient limitation even if soil stocks were adequate, because they lack the root architecture to access those nutrients independently.

**Medium-term: Trophic cascade.** Declining producer biomass reduces energy input to the entire grazer pathway. Consumer populations decline. The system's total energy throughput drops because the tap (solar flux captured by producers) has been throttled by nutrient limitation, while the drain (metabolic costs) continues.

**Long-term: System state shift.** The ecosystem shifts from a nutrient-cycling steady state to a nutrient-accumulating state. Carbon and nutrients pile up in dead organic matter rather than flowing through living systems. Soil becomes increasingly oligotrophic (nutrient-poor in available forms, even as total nutrient content in locked organic matter may be high). The system converges toward a low-productivity, low-diversity state dominated by whatever producers can survive without mycorrhizal support and with minimal nutrient cycling.

**Loss of diversity maintenance.** Without parasitic fungi enforcing density-dependent mortality, dominant species face less biotic resistance to competitive exclusion. Species diversity declines as competitive dominants monopolise resources without pathogen-mediated setbacks.

The overall pattern: removing fungi doesn't eliminate energy from the system — solar flux still arrives. It breaks the nutrient loops that allow that energy to be captured and recirculated. The system doesn't run out of energy; it runs out of the molecular building blocks needed to capture energy.
