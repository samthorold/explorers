# Cannibalism and Kin

Every heterotroph eats organic matter, and the nearest organic matter is often its own species: neighbours, eggs, young, parents. Cannibalism (intraspecific predation) is what happens when the consumer–resource interaction described in [trophic roles](trophic-roles.md) turns inward. Kin-directed consumption is the special case where the victim is a relative. This document covers:

- how common cannibalism is;
- the forms it takes;
- what drives it and what limits it;
- how organisms avoid eating their own relatives;
- what it does to population dynamics.

The through-line is spatial. Offspring start life next to their parents and siblings. So a consumer that cannot tell kin from non-kin meets its relatives first, and selection on the cost of eating them shapes recognition, timing, dispersal and parental care. The spatial side of that story connects to [spatial ecology](spatial-ecology.md) and to the dispersal section of [life history theory](life-history-theory.md).

## How common it is

Cannibalism is **taxonomically widespread, but in most species it is a small share of the diet.** Both halves matter.

**Widespread.** Fox (1975) called it "a normal phenomenon in many natural populations", not a laboratory artefact. Polis (1981) compiled more than 900 papers reporting intraspecific predation in about 1,300 species:

- egg cannibalism in more than 80 families, "in practically every major group of egg-laying animal";
- cannibalism of newborn young in more than 80 families.

Group surveys:

| group | extent | notes | source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mammals | 75 species in 7 orders | about a quarter of lion cubs are killed and eaten by older lions | Polis, Myers & Hess 1984; Polis 1981 |
| Insects, noncarnivorous orders | 130 species | adults are rarely victims (5.7 % of species), mostly when defenceless, e.g. just after moulting; 60 % of reports are from the laboratory and 20 % from the field only | Richardson et al. 2010 |
| Birds | comparatively rare | concentrated in species that are carnivorous, colonial and dismember prey (raptors, seabirds) | Stanback & Koenig 1992 |

It is common in fish, amphibian larvae, arachnids and insects. It is least common in herbivorous birds and mammals.

**A small share of the diet.** Where it has been measured, conspecifics are usually a minor fraction of intake:

- 0.1–9.1 % of prey items in three example species (Polis 1981);
- 7–28 % of those same diets by weight, since conspecific victims are large meals;
- 14 % of adult prey in the scorpion *Paruroctonus mesaensis*.

Extremes exist. Large cannibal fish take about half their diet from their own species. A few specialist herring gulls each ate 2–5 % of a colony's chicks (Polis 1981).

So the everyday intuition that cannibalism is rare is right in one sense: few meals are conspecific. It is wrong in another: few lineages never do it.

## Forms

### Size-structured cannibalism

Most cannibalism is big eating small (Polis 1981). Victims are eggs, larvae, juveniles or the newly moulted, all defenceless stages, which keeps the risk to the cannibal low.

In Eurasian perch (*Perca fluviatilis*), adults eat young-of-year. That interacts with competition between age classes to drive population dynamics. After a cannibal die-off, the few large survivors gain heavily from strong year classes, growing and reproducing faster (Persson, Byström & Wahlström 2000).

### Filial cannibalism: parents eating their young

It is best documented in fish with paternal egg care (Manica 2002). There are two forms:

- **Total filial cannibalism** eats the whole brood. It is an investment only in the parent's future reproduction.
- **Partial filial cannibalism** eats part of it, and affects both current and future broods.

It is condition- and value-dependent. Bluegill fathers in poor condition eat part of the brood. Males with small broods eat all of it. Males that have been cuckolded more cull more, but only after hatching, once fry odour reveals paternity (Neff 2003). In the blenny *Rhabdoblennius nitidus*, whole-brood cannibalism belongs to males with low androgen levels (11-ketotestosterone) and is tied to the brood cycle (Takegaki et al. 2023).

### Sibling cannibalism and siblicide

Siblings are each other's nearest competitors and nearest food.

- **Sand tiger shark (*Carcharias taurus*).** In each uterus the first embryo to reach a threshold size eats all its smaller siblings. Genetic data show that this removes some sires' offspring entirely: 60 % of females mated with more than one male, but only 40 % of litters had more than one father (Chapman et al. 2013).
- **Ladybird (*Harmonia axyridis*).** Larvae that eat sibling eggs grow larger and survive better, and the benefit is clearest when aphids are scarce (Osawa 1992).

The broader theory of sibling rivalry and siblicide is in Mock & Parker (1997).

### Offspring eating their parents

Yes, young do eat their parents. Matriphagy (offspring eating their mother) is known from spiders:

- **In *Stegodyphus lineatus*,** the mother first loses 41 % of her body mass regurgitating food to the brood. The young then eat a further 54 %, so about 95 % of her mass ends up in her offspring (Salomon, Schneider & Lubin 2005).
- **In *Amaurobius ferox*,** young that eat their mother survive better and moult earlier. Mothers experimentally removed before matriphagy could produce a second clutch with 33 % more viable young, so matriphagy trades the mother's future reproduction for the current brood (Kim, Roland & Horel 2000).
- **The crab spider *Diaea ergandros*** shows the same self-sacrificing care (Evans 1998).

A non-lethal version exists in the caecilian amphibian *Boulengerula taitanus*. The young use specialised teeth to peel and eat their mother's modified outer skin. The mother loses about 14 % of her body mass while the young grow about 11 % in length (Kupfer et al. 2006). This is parental provisioning, not cannibalism in the lethal sense.

These cases are the inverse of filial cannibalism. The parent's body is the final reproductive investment, and it is spent on offspring that are all its own. That is kin consumption *favoured* by kin selection.

### Sexual cannibalism

Mostly females eating males: 88 % of sex-differentiated cases in Polis's (1981) survey. In a field study of the praying mantis *Mantis religiosa*, females ate the male in 31 % of observed matings (Lawrence 1992).

## Drivers

- **Density.** Polis (1981) found 65 reports of cannibalism rising with crowding, and more than 40 describing it as a density-dependent regulator. In insects it is attributed more often to density than to density-independent factors. For example, the share of *Labidomera* eggs eaten rises with clutch size (Richardson et al. 2010). Density raises encounter rates, and the nearest conspecific is a likely meal.
- **Hunger.** Hunger triggers searching, lowers the attack threshold and widens the range of acceptable prey (Polis 1981). Hungry spadefoot carnivores also become less discriminating about kin (Pfennig, Reeve & Sherman 1993).
- **Vulnerability and asymmetry.** Moulting, diseased and parasitised individuals are taken. So are individuals out of step in development, where a head start becomes a size advantage. Heat and drought raise cannibalism too (Richardson et al. 2010).
- **Energy recovery by parents.** Filial cannibalism recovers the energy a parent has sunk into a brood of low value (Manica 2002; Neff 2003).

## Costs and limits

Three costs keep cannibalism in check.

1. **Inclusive fitness.** Eating a relative destroys copies of one's own genes. By Hamilton's (1964) rule, eating kin pays only when the energy gained exceeds the victim's reproductive value weighted by relatedness. Kin cannibalism is therefore selected against more strongly than cannibalism of non-kin (Pfennig 1997).
2. **Pathogens.** Conspecifics share parasites and susceptibilities. Tiger salamander cannibals fed diseased conspecifics survived to metamorphosis less often, and grew less, than those fed diseased prey of another species (Pfennig, Ho & Hoffman 1998). Diseases spread mainly by cannibalism need shared feeding on a victim. They are uncommon even though cannibalism is common (Rudolf & Antonovics 2007). In tiger salamanders, kin selection rather than disease avoidance best explains kin discrimination (Pfennig, Collins & Ziemba 1999).
3. **Injury.** Fighting an equal is dangerous. This is why cannibalism concentrates on defenceless stages (Polis 1981).

## How organisms avoid eating kin

Offspring start next to their family, so kin-blind feeding falls disproportionately on kin. Organisms have evolved several independent solutions.

**Recognition by cue.**

- **Spadefoot tadpoles (*Spea bombifrons*).** Carnivore-morph tadpoles nip a conspecific, eat it if it is not a sibling, and release it unharmed if it is (Pfennig, Reeve & Sherman 1993).
- **Tiger salamander larvae.** Both morphs prefer less-related victims and can tell siblings from cousins. Blocking the nostrils removes the discrimination, so smell is the cue (Pfennig, Sherman & Collins 1994).

**Development that depends on kin.**

- **Tiger salamanders.** Larvae raised in mixed broods develop into the cannibal morph more often and earlier than larvae raised with siblings only (Pfennig & Collins 1993).
- **Spadefoot toads (*Spea multiplicata*).** The same holds for the carnivore morph (Pfennig & Frankino 1997).

Here the capacity for cannibalism is only built when non-kin are around to eat.

**Timing.**

- **Burying beetles.** Females kill larvae that arrive before their own eggs could have hatched, then switch to caring for larvae (Müller & Eggert 1990).
- **Male mice.** Most males kill pups 1–4 days after mating. From 12 to 50 days after mating the same males are inhibited and act parentally, and the switch is triggered by ejaculation (vom Saal 1985). This is infanticide, which need not involve eating the young.

In both, a timer does the work of recognition: "young that appear when mine should are mine".

**Recognition by location.** Many parents treat whatever is in the nest as their own.

- **Bank swallows.** Parents accept swapped chicks up to about 15 days old and begin rejecting them at 16–17 days, when chicks start to move between nests (Beecher, Beecher & Lumpkin 1981).
- **The crab spider *Diaea*.** Mothers accept unrelated spiderlings at high nest density, but hunt preferentially for their own young (Evans 1998).

Location works as a recognition rule only because offspring are where their parents put them.

**Spatial separation.**

- **Egg placement.** Ladybird females (*Adalia bipunctata*) avoid laying eggs where conspecific larvae have left tracks, which carry a species-specific deterrent. That lowers the risk to their eggs (Doumbia, Hemptinne & Dixon 1998).
- **Dispersal.** More generally, dispersal separates relatives (next section).

**Evolved restraint in viscous populations.** Indian meal moth (*Plodia interpunctella*) larvae were kept in more viscous food, so they moved less and relatives stayed clustered. Those lines evolved *lower* cannibalism, as kin-selection theory predicts (Boots et al. 2021). This is the experimental case closest to "a kin-blind consumer in a poorly dispersed population". Selection on the cannibal itself reduces its appetite for neighbours.

## Dispersal and kin competition

Dispersal is the general answer to the problem of relatives as neighbours. Hamilton & May (1977) showed that dispersal is favoured even in uniform, stable habitats. Offspring that stay behind compete with their siblings, so a parent gains by sending some away, even at a cost to the population as a whole.

- **Extensions.** The result was extended to diploid and haplodiploid genetics and to maternal versus offspring control of dispersal (Taylor 1988). The stable dispersal rate depends on the cost of dispersing, relatedness among offspring and the cost of inbreeding (Gandon 1999).
- **Review.** Ronce (2007) reviews dispersal evolution.
- **Individual-based evidence.** In an individual-based model where kin competition emerges on its own rather than being imposed as average relatedness, it measurably raises evolved dispersal (Bach et al. 2006).

Cannibalism of kin is the sharpest form of kin competition: the relative is not competing for the food, it *is* the food. The same logic applies with more force.

**Population viscosity.** Limited dispersal raises relatedness among neighbours (Hamilton 1964). But the same viscosity makes those relatives the main competitors:

- In the simplest models the relatedness benefit and the kin-competition cost cancel exactly (Taylor 1992; Wilson, Pollock & Dugatkin 1992).
- Kin-directed helping can evolve only if dispersal is uncoupled from local population regulation (Queller 1992).

The corollary for harm is direct: in a viscous population, *indiscriminate* harm, including predation, lands mostly on relatives.

**A spatial analogue without cannibalism: Janzen–Connell.** Seedlings near their parent tree die at higher rates, because host-specific seed predators, herbivores and pathogens build up around the parent (Janzen 1970; Connell 1971). A meta-analysis of experiments confirms lower seed and seedling survival near same-species adults and at high same-species density (Comita et al. 2014). The parent does not kill its offspring. Its enemies do. The outcome still has the shape of the problem above: being born where your parent is carries a mortality cost, and dispersal escapes it.

## Beyond animals

Self/non-self and kin discrimination, and self-consumption, occur well outside animals.

- **Plants.** Sea rocket (*Cakile edentula*) grows more roots competing with strangers than with siblings, a result consistent with kin selection (Dudley & File 2007).
- **Fungi.** When hyphae of genetically different colonies fuse, heterokaryon incompatibility triggers rapid programmed death of the fused cells. Hyphal networks form only between colonies identical at all the self/non-self recognition (*het*) loci (Glass & Dementhon 2006). Fungal colonies do not merge with, or feed on, non-self through fusion.
- **Bacteria.** Starving *Bacillus subtilis* cells that have begun to sporulate secrete a killing factor and a signalling protein. These make sister cells that have not yet committed to sporulation lyse, and the killers feed on the released nutrients. This delays their own irreversible commitment to spores (González-Pastor, Hobbs & Losick 2003). Here cannibalism of close kin, clonal siblings, is a starvation strategy.
- **Social amoebae.** Genetically different *Dictyostelium discoideum* strains segregate during multicellular development, and the more so the more distant they are (Ostrowski et al. 2008).
- **Ciliates.** In dense, fast-growing cultures, *Blepharisma* forms giant cannibal cells that eat much smaller members of their own species (Dawson 1929). Size asymmetry is again what makes a conspecific edible.

## Consequences for population dynamics

Cannibalism is neither simply stabilising nor destabilising. Claessen, de Roos & Persson (2004) break it into four components:

- the killing of victims;
- the energy the cannibal gains;
- size-dependence;
- the intraspecific competition that removing victims relieves.

Different combinations of these components produce five kinds of effect:

1. **Regulation.** Cannibalism is a density-dependent mortality term. Ricker's (1954) dome-shaped stock–recruitment curve, `R = aS·e^(−bS)`, comes from such a term. Ricker named cannibalism as one of several plausible mechanisms, especially for predatory fish in small lakes.
2. **Destabilisation.** Cannibalism can drive cycles and chaos.
   - **Flour beetles.** In the flour beetle *Tribolium*, egg and pupal cannibalism are the nonlinearity in the larva–pupa–adult model. Experimentally changing adult mortality moved laboratory populations from equilibrium to cycles to chaos, as the model predicted (Costantino et al. 1995, 1997).
   - **Size-structured fish.** Voracious cannibalism combined with competition gives large cycles and a two-peaked size distribution: many stunted fish and a few giant cannibals (Claessen, de Roos & Persson 2000).
3. **Stabilisation.** Cannibalism can damp cycles that other interactions cause. Moderate cannibalism relieves competition between age groups and lets many age groups coexist (Claessen, de Roos & Persson 2000).
4. **Bistability.** A cannibalistic population can settle into one of two alternative stable states depending on its starting point (Claessen, de Roos & Persson 2004).
5. **Changes to size structure.** In a perch lake, ten years of data showed an internally driven switch from stunted fish to "gigantic cannibals". It cascaded through the zooplankton and phytoplankton below (Persson et al. 2003).

**The life-boat mechanism.** Suppose juveniles feed on a resource adults cannot reach. Then adults that eat juveniles use that resource indirectly. A cannibalistic population can then persist when adult food alone would not sustain a non-cannibal one, provided one eaten juvenile yields more than one new offspring at low density (van den Bosch, de Roos & Gabriel 1988). Here cannibalism is a survival channel, not a drain.

## Feedback loops

- **Density → cannibalism → density (balancing).** Crowding raises encounter rates and cannibalism, which removes recruits and relieves crowding. This is the regulatory loop behind Ricker-type recruitment.
- **Recruitment → cannibal food → cannibal fecundity → recruitment (reinforcing, delayed).** A strong year class feeds the large cannibals, which then reproduce more and produce the next strong year class. The delay and overshoot are how cannibalism destabilises (the perch and *Tribolium* cycles).
- **Limited dispersal → kin clustered → kin-blind feeding hits kin → selection for recognition, restraint or dispersal (balancing, evolutionary).** Where offspring stay near family, indiscriminate consumption costs inclusive fitness. Selection responds on whichever axis is available: recognition cues, timing rules, reduced appetite (Boots et al. 2021) or wider dispersal (Hamilton & May 1977).
- **Hunger → less discrimination → more kin eaten.** Starvation weakens kin discrimination (Pfennig, Reeve & Sherman 1993). So in lean times the protection kin recognition gives erodes when it matters most.

## ABM and computational literature

Much of the theory above is individual-based or physiologically structured:

- **Size-structured fish.** The dwarfs-and-giants cycles (Claessen, de Roos & Persson 2000) and the whole-lake perch cascade (Persson et al. 2003) come from models that track individual size and energy. They reproduce field dynamics that unstructured models cannot.
- **Flour beetles.** The *Tribolium* larva–pupa–adult model is a rare case of chaotic dynamics predicted by a model and then confirmed in the laboratory (Costantino et al. 1997).
- **Dispersal.** In individual-based dispersal models, kin competition that emerges from local relatedness raises evolved dispersal. Local extinction or dispersal cost, where they dominate, can mask the effect (Bach et al. 2006).
- **Viscous populations.** Simulations of purely viscous populations show that high relatedness and local population regulation go together, so indiscriminate altruism does not evolve (Wilson, Pollock & Dugatkin 1992). The same logic makes indiscriminate harm fall on relatives.

**A gap.** The research for this document found no artificial-life or agent-based study where predation that cannot tell kin from non-kin, among clonal or related agents, suppresses the spread of a lineage or selects for kin recognition. The closest evidence is:

- the *Plodia* selection experiment (Boots et al. 2021);
- the salamander and spadefoot kin-discrimination work (Pfennig 1997);
- the viscous-population theory above.
