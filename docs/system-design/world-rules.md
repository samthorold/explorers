# World Rules

The immutable physics of the simulation. These define what the world is made of and how it behaves before any agent strategy, population dynamic, or emergent pattern is considered. An agent dropped into this world faces these constraints unconditionally.

This document is built on the ecological ground truths in [docs/ecology/](../ecology/) — observable properties of real ecosystems that we take as given. It informs but does not prescribe how the implementation encodes these rules in code.

## Stocks

The world has two currencies. Energy is the primary currency — it powers all processes and is the currency of metabolism. In addition, the world has a single nutrient that agents require alongside energy. An agent's nutrient demand — how much nutrient it needs per unit energy — is derived from its trait vector: more capable agents need more nutrient. Together, energy and nutrient are the building blocks of every agent.

### Energy stocks

Energy flows through the system in one direction: from source to sink. It does not cycle.

Within a living agent, energy exists in two forms: **reserve** (metabolic fuel — the operating account) and **structure** (embodied biomass — the physical body). Reserve fluctuates rapidly as income and costs are applied each tick. Structure accumulates slowly through growth and is depleted by consumption from other agents. The distinction follows Kooijman's (2010) Dynamic Energy Budget theory, where reserve and structure are separate state variables with different dynamics.

Reserve is internally partitioned into two sub-accounts: an **unallocated** portion that funds metabolism, growth, and all activity costs, and a **reproductive allocation** that earmarks energy committed to reproduction by kappa at the moment surplus is generated (see flow 9). Both sub-accounts are reserve — they are not a third form of energy. The reproductive allocation is a labelled fraction of reserve, accumulated tick over tick from the (1 − kappa) share of surplus, and drawn down only when a reproductive event fires (flow 4). The partition is bookkeeping that records a prior allocation decision; total energy in the agent is still reserve + structure, and conservation treats the agent's total energy as the sum of unallocated reserve, reproductive allocation, and structure.

| Stock | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Solar flux** | Source (inexhaustible) | Energy enters the system here. The flux is constant — the same amount of energy is available every tick. All temporal variation in the system is endogenous. |
| **Living agents (reserve)** | Internal | The metabolic fuel held by living organisms. Reserve increases through energy acquisition (photosynthesis, consumption) and decreases through metabolic costs, growth, and reproduction. Reserve is the central clearing account — all energy income and expenditure flows through it. |
| **Living agents (structure)** | Internal | The embodied biomass energy of living organisms. Structure accumulates when an agent allocates reserve surplus to growth (a lossy conversion). Structure is depleted when another agent consumes the body. Structure is not a searched world parameter or a heritable trait — it is a state variable provisioned at birth from the parental energy investment (see flow 9 and the `offspring_structure_fraction` parameter) and grows over the agent's lifetime. Living agents also accumulate somatic wear — see below. |
| **Carcasses** | Internal | Energy held by dead organisms. When an agent dies, its structure (embodied biomass) and nutrient become a carcass at the same location on the surface. A carcass's energy content reflects the agent's accumulated structure at death. Carcasses hold energy indefinitely: only a heterotroph's drain returns it. Their nutrient leaches slowly to the pool (*Carcass energy decays only through agents; carcass nutrient leaches*). |
| **Heat** | Sink (inexhaustible) | Energy leaves the system here, permanently. Every lossy process — metabolism, trophic transfer, growth conversion, reproduction inefficiency — sends energy to this sink. It does not return. |

### Nutrient

Unlike energy, nutrient cycles. It is not consumed and lost — it passes through agents and returns to the environment when agents die and are decomposed. The total amount of nutrient in the system is conserved across all pools (available, living agents, carcasses, unavailable).

The system tracks a single nutrient. This nutrient enters the living system only through uptake from the available pool — a process that requires sustained contact with the substrate (see flow 2). Nutrient is **matter embodied in biomass**: within a living agent it sits in two places mirroring the two forms of energy — a **free store** (unbound, available, the analogue of reserve) and the amount **bound in structure** (`structure × demand`, the analogue of structure itself), locked into the body until that structure is removed. The free store is further partitioned, exactly as reserve is: an unearmarked portion that growth may bind into structure, and a **reproductive nutrient** earmark — the (1 − kappa) share of each tick's nutrient income, whether absorbed from the pool (flow 2) or ingested by consumption (flow 3) — that growth cannot touch and that funds reproduction (flow 4). The split is route-agnostic, mirroring the energy side: kappa partitions every unit of acquired nutrient the same way it partitions surplus reserve (flow 9), regardless of which flow delivered it. Nutrient leaves a living agent two ways: grazing removes the nutrient bound in the structure eaten (flow 3), and death converts everything — free store, earmark, and bound nutrient — into a carcass (flow 6).

**Stoichiometric demand.** Different agents need nutrient in different amounts relative to energy, depending on their traits. An agent's nutrient demand is derived from its trait vector — more capable agents (higher trait values) require more nutrient per unit energy. When the nutrient-to-energy ratio in food does not match the consumer's demand, the consumer retains only what it needs and excretes the excess nutrient immediately to the available pool. The limiting currency (energy or nutrient) constrains how much of the consumed material the consumer can actually use. Nutrient **co-limits growth** — growth binds free nutrient into the body, so it stalls when free nutrient runs out — and **gates reproduction** through the reproductive-nutrient earmark; metabolism is unaffected.

**Differential limitation.** Even with a single nutrient, the system creates differential limitation across the surface. The available pool varies spatially — some locations are nutrient-rich, others nutrient-poor. Agents at nutrient-poor locations may be nutrient-limited (can acquire energy but not enough nutrient to reproduce), while agents at nutrient-rich locations may be energy-limited (have plenty of nutrient but insufficient energy). This spatial variation in which currency is limiting creates context-dependent fitness — different strategies are favoured at different locations.

### Nutrient pools

Nutrients exist in four pools, analogous to the energy stocks:

| Pool | Description |
|---|---|
| **Available** | Nutrient in the environment that is biologically accessible — extractable by agents through nutrient uptake. Distributed spatially across the substrate. Co-located agents share the available pool proportionally (like light competition). |
| **Living agents** | Nutrient held by living agents — partly bound in structure (`structure × demand`, matter locked in the body) and partly held free (an unearmarked store growth can bind, plus a reproductive-nutrient earmark reserved for offspring). Metabolism does not release nutrient; it leaves only by grazing (bound portion of structure eaten) or death (all of it). |
| **Carcasses** | Nutrient locked in dead matter. Released by decomposition: the consumer retains what its stoichiometric demand calls for and excretes the excess to the available pool at the carcass (flow 3). Its leachable nutrient also leaches to that pool with no agent involved (*Carcass energy decays only through agents; carcass nutrient leaches*). The nutrient content of a carcass reflects the content of the agent that died. |
| **Unavailable** | Nutrient locked in forms that are not biologically accessible — bound in rock, occluded in substrate chemistry, or in chemical states that no agent can process. These pools change only through geological-timescale processes. |

### Conservation laws

**Energy conservation.** At any point in time:

> Total system energy = Σ(living agent reserve) + Σ(living agent structure) + Σ(carcass energy)

where each living agent's reserve includes both its unallocated portion and its accumulated reproductive allocation — the (1 − kappa) earmark is a partition of reserve, not a separate stock.

The change in total system energy per tick equals:

> ΔE = photosynthetic input − total dissipation to heat

Energy is neither created nor destroyed within the system. Every flow is accounted for. Reserve-to-structure conversion (growth) is internal to a living agent and does not change total system energy, but the conversion is lossy — the difference dissipates to heat.

**Nutrient conservation.** For the nutrient:

> Total nutrient = available pool + Σ(living agent nutrient) + Σ(carcass nutrient) + unavailable pool

where each living agent's nutrient is the sum of its free store, its reproductive-nutrient earmark, and the nutrient bound in its structure — the earmark and the bound portion are partitions of the agent's nutrient, not separate stocks. Growth binds free nutrient into structure (an internal move, conserving the agent's total), and reproduction transfers reproductive nutrient from parent to offspring (conserving the living pool's total).

Nutrient is neither created nor destroyed. It cycles between pools. The unavailable pool changes only through geological-timescale processes (weathering, deposition), not through biological activity.

**Founders bind nutrient at world creation.** The whole initial nutrient pool starts in the available pool, spread evenly over the nutrient cells. Founders are born with structure, so each one binds `structure × demand` of nutrient, and that nutrient is drawn from the available pool, not conjured. Total nutrient at creation is therefore the initial pool. Founders draw in id order, each from the cell it stands on. The available pool cannot go negative, so when a founder's cell can't cover its draw (a tight founding patch over a small pool, #612), the cell is emptied and the shortfall comes from the **nearest cells outward**. The draw takes the ring of eight cells around it first, then the ring beyond that, and so on, wrapping over the torus edge. Within a ring the cells are taken row by row and column by column, and each is emptied before the next is touched. Nearest-first matches what a founding body does: a growing body draws on the substrate next to it, not on a distant cell picked by a global rule. It also keeps the depletion local and visible, and it is deterministic. Where a founder's own cell covers its draw, the binding is exactly the in-place subtraction, so such worlds are unchanged by the rule.

If the whole pool cannot cover every founder's seed body, **founding is co-limited by nutrient**, as growth is (flow 9, Liebig's law of the minimum). Every founder commits the same share, `pool ÷ Σ bound`, of the energy it would have put into structure. The founders are smaller and together bind the pool out exactly. The energy their structure would have taken stays in their reserve, as energy that growth cannot match with nutrient stays in reserve. Energy is conserved, and every founder is still embodied while the pool holds any nutrient at all; a zero pool founds structureless bodies, which is the kill switch a zero pool already is. The alternative, rejecting the world, would turn a legal parameter corner into a crash, and nothing about such a world is unphysical: it is a world whose founders arrive into scarce matter. (The search box holds the pool far above any founding's bound nutrient, so a search world never reaches this case. It is a guard for hand-authored and future recipes.)

If either conservation law fails, the world is broken.

### Carcass energy decays only through agents; carcass nutrient leaches

A carcass's **energy** (its structure) never decays on its own. Only a heterotroph's drain returns it to the living system (flow 3), so decomposer strategies are structurally necessary: they are the only route back for the energy fixed in the dead. A carcass's **nutrient** also leaves it without an agent. It **leaches** slowly to the available pool at the carcass's cell. The principle is: **all transformation of energy requires an agent.** Nutrient is conserved and moves between stores, and one of its moves needs no organism.

This is the split the domain makes. The carbon and polymers of dead matter are broken down almost entirely by organisms: bacteria, fungi and invertebrates digest it outside their bodies, and that work is what makes decomposition fall to specialists ([trophic roles](../ecology/trophic-roles.md), *Why decomposition falls to specialists*). Soluble nutrient, by contrast, leaves dead matter by leaching whether or not a decomposer is present. Leaching can account for up to a third of early mass loss in a standard litter-bag assay (Lind et al. 2022). Photodegradation and fire are further abiotic routes ([stability and resilience](../ecology/stability-and-resilience.md), *Real litter always has an unconditional exit*).

The consequence is that lockup is partial and slow, as it is in the domain, not an absorbing state. A world without decomposers still starves of the energy locked in its dead and loses the recycling that drains provide. But its pool is not emptied for good by the first wave of deaths, because the dead give back their soluble nutrient at a rate no failure of the living can stop. The domain's closest analogue to lockup, paludification under moss and peat, is a slowing of both abiotic and microbial release, with productivity falling by half or more until fire resets the store. It is not a store that nothing can ever open. Detritus stabilises a food web through donor control, a release that follows the size of the pile (Moore et al. 2004). A release that depends wholly on drainers being present and in reach loses that control exactly when drainers are scarce, which is where #677's atlas's fragility sits ([693-fragility-audit.md](../research/693-fragility-audit.md): flip rate rises with the carcass axis, ρ +0.37).

Leaching is not a safety valve. A valve is a release tuned to rescue worlds that the domain does not have. Leaching is a domain flow with its own form and its own rate, and it moves only nutrient. The energy side of the dead stays wholly agentic, so the decomposer niche keeps its reason to exist: it is the only way to eat the pile. A leached carcass carries less nutrient per unit structure, so it is a smaller prize to a nutrient-short drainer (flow 3's deficit term) and the same meal to a heterotroph feeding for energy. Leaching therefore shifts the pile's value toward the decomposers it is food for.

**The form.** Only a carcass's **leachable nutrient** leaches: the free store and the reproductive earmark the dead agent carried. The nutrient bound in its structure leaves only with the structure, through a drain. That is the domain's split between soluble nutrient and nutrient built into polymers. It is also what makes a carcass unlike a body. A carcass carries its dead agent's whole store and earmark, so it is far richer in nutrient per unit structure than any body ratio (flow 3; #670 measured carcass bites at 7–10 nutrient per unit structure, drain-weighted, against body ratios under 1). Leaching relaxes it toward its structure's own ratio, and what stays locked where nothing drains is the bound fraction alone. Each tick a carcass leaches `λ × leachable nutrient` to the available pool at its cell, where `λ` is the **leaching rate**, a world parameter. The law is first order because the domain's leaching follows the soluble store, and a release that follows the size of the store is the donor control that stabilises detritus (Moore et al. 2004). It is smooth in `λ` and in the store, with no threshold. The leachable nutrient needs no new state: it is the carcass's nutrient above what its remaining structure binds at the dead agent's stoichiometric ratio, `max(0, nutrient − structure × ratio)`, so a carcass leaches down to its structure's ratio and stops. A drain still takes nutrient in proportion to the structure it removes, as now. The stepper's default is `λ = 0`, where the leaching term is skipped and every recipe that predates leaching is unchanged bit for bit, as with size-scaled uptake and the network. The worlds genesis searches run at a fixed `λ = 0.0025` (below). **The rate.** The domain bounds the rate: `λ_max = ln 2 / t*`, the rate at which a carcass leaches half its leachable store in the time a drainer takes to reach it. `t*` is the median time from death to first drain over carcasses a drainer has to find, which leaves out those bitten to death, whose drainer is already on them and bites again the next tick. Any faster leaching would empty the soluble store before decomposers could matter, which would undo the split above. The settled half gives `t* = 68` ticks, so `λ_max = 0.01`. Three-quarters of such carcasses are never drained, so the median over drained ones understates how long a carcass waits, which makes this an upper bound. Genesis's worlds run at **`λ = λ_max / 4 = 0.0025`**, a half-life of about 277 ticks, fixed and not searched. It is not 0 because real carcasses leach: a world without leaching locks every unfound carcass's soluble nutrient away for good, which the domain does not do. It is a quarter of the top because the response to `λ` is steeply concave. The carcasses that lock nutrient up are the ones no drainer ever finds, their clock is the run's length, and even a slow rate empties them. By `λ_max/4` the response has nearly saturated, and faster rates change little. It is not searched because genesis does not select it. Robustness-scored searches spread `λ` as they spread any coordinate they do not select on, and the worlds nearest `λ = 0` were if anything the most robust (#686, #711). A dimension the search does not select on only adds noise to the search, the reason the cross-trait cost is latent too. Leaching still helps the worlds it acts on: held fixed, the committed atlas's worlds lose most of their lockup at this rate (below). The search simply finds other ways around lockup, so leaching belongs in the physics, not in the box. What it cannot release is nutrient bound in structure, which leaves only through a drain: the lockup that persists at high `λ` sits in worlds with high stoichiometric ratios. *Measured (#700, 2026-10-06; [`700-leaching-calibration.md`](../research/700-leaching-calibration.md)): on the committed atlas, nutrient-lockup seeds fall from 19 of 495 at `λ = 0` to 7 at `λ_max/16`, 2 at `λ_max/4` and 1 at `λ_max`, and monoculture seeds rise from 6 to 10–17 at every `λ > 0`.*

### Somatic wear

Living agents degrade over time. Every functional capability — photosynthetic apparatus, locomotion machinery, sensory organs, digestive systems — accumulates wear through use and through the baseline cost of maintaining complex machinery. This is the disposable soma principle (Kirkwood 1977): an organism could theoretically maintain its body indefinitely, but the energy required for perfect repair is energy unavailable for reproduction. The body is disposable because investing in immortality is a losing strategy when extrinsic mortality exists.

Wear accumulates per functional trait, not as a single aggregate. An agent that photosynthesizes heavily wears out its photosynthetic apparatus faster than an agent in shade. An agent that sprints wears out its locomotion faster than a sessile one. The rate of wear accumulation emerges from the agent's trait-space position and activity — not from flat rate parameters.

Wear reduces trait effectiveness. Small wear barely affects output — biological systems have redundancy and over-provisioning. But wear compounds, and later increments are increasingly costly. An aging producer captures less light. An aging consumer catches prey less efficiently. An aging agent senses less, moves slower, processes food less effectively.

Agents invest in somatic maintenance to counteract wear. The level of investment is governed by kappa — the heritable fraction of surplus energy allocated to soma vs. reproduction. Some lineages evolve high kappa (long-lived, slow-reproducing), others evolve low kappa (short-lived, fast-reproducing). Somatic maintenance is a whole-organism investment: an agent does not selectively repair one organ while neglecting another. The energy allocated to maintenance competes directly with the energy available for reproduction — this is the core trade-off that Kirkwood identified.

The balance between wear accumulation and repair must produce three properties:
- **Stable equilibrium.** For a given kappa and activity level, an agent reaches a steady-state wear level where repair balances accumulation. At equilibrium, effective traits are degraded but still functional.
- **Activity-dependent equilibrium.** Higher throughput (more photosynthesis, more consumption, more movement) increases the equilibrium wear level. Busier agents age faster, but stabilise at a lower level of function rather than spiralling to death.
- **Trait-space-derived lifespan.** An agent's position on the survive-vs-reproduce axis determines its lifespan. High kappa produces long-lived agents with low equilibrium wear. Low kappa produces short-lived agents with high equilibrium wear. The lifespan gradient is continuous, not binary.

**Use-dependent wear is one coefficient over three couplings.** The committed rule accrues per-tick use-dependent wear on each functional trait as `use_wear_rate × usage`, where usage is *energy captured* for autotrophy, *energy drained* for heterotrophy, and *distance moved* for mobility. Wear is an energy (repair is 1:1 with energy, flow 9), so the first two couplings are dimensionless (`E/E`) while the third is `E/L` — one coefficient cannot carry both. The rule therefore carries three implied unit constants, one per coupling, each `1.0` in today's units: `u_WA` (per energy captured), `u_WH` (per energy drained), `u_WM` (per distance moved) — named in code as `units::USE_WEAR_PER_ENERGY_CAPTURED`, `units::USE_WEAR_PER_ENERGY_DRAINED`, `units::USE_WEAR_PER_DISTANCE_MOVED` (see *Unit anchors* below). The inhomogeneity is latent in practice: `use_wear_rate` is `0` on the search baseline and on the recipes that exercise the current verdicts (example4, 9–13); the older recipes example1–3, 5, 7 and 8 set it to `0.01`. Whether to resolve it — by splitting the coefficient into a per-energy and a per-distance rate, or by converting distance moved to the energy it already costs (`distance × movement_cost_coefficient × structure`) before it enters wear — is a design decision to be taken with the designer, not one the committed rules make ([`440-dimensionless-groups.md`](../research/440-dimensionless-groups.md), smell S2).

**Wear is always on.** Every world runs with a nonzero wear rate. Wear is part of the physics, not a world switch. Without it nothing senesces: producers die only of starvation, structural loss or consumption, lifespan no longer follows from kappa, and the survivorship continuum and demographic turnover in the absence of predation ([expected properties](expected-properties.md)) have no mechanism. Reference modes depend on it too. A colonising cohort overshoots because it ages and dies together ([reference modes](reference-modes.md), *Colonisation overshoot*). Without senescence, an even-aged cohort only self-thins: crowded individuals starve, survivors grow, and biomass keeps rising. An instrument whose readings are valid only without wear has a limit on that instrument. It is not a reason to remove wear from the worlds it reads. How large the wear rate is, and whether genesis searches it or fixes it, is a calibration question. Whether wear acts at all is not.

Behavioural traits — kappa, fecundity — do not wear. They are allocation parameters, not physical machinery. An old organism is less capable, not less decisive.

Identity-determining thresholds use an agent's nominal (unworn) traits. An agent born mobile is ecologically mobile for its entire life, even as its effective mobility degrades with age. Wear degrades performance, not identity. An aging wolf does not become a plant.

**Offspring are born with zero wear.** This is the evolutionary rationale for reproduction: it resets the soma. The germline is maintained at higher fidelity than the soma. A parent's worn-out body produces a brand-new body. The information (heritable traits) persists in pristine hardware (the offspring). This asymmetry between germline and soma is what makes the disposable soma trade-off coherent — repair your body, or build a new one.

Grounded in the disposable soma theory (Kirkwood 1977) and antagonistic pleiotropy (Williams 1957). Real organisms senesce — decline in physiological function with age — because energy invested in somatic repair competes with reproduction. The rate of senescence is calibrated to the extrinsic mortality rate: organisms in high-mortality environments evolve faster senescence because investing in longevity beyond the expected lifespan is wasteful (Reznick et al. 2004, Austad 1993). This produces the survivorship curve continuum: Type I (low early mortality, concentrated late-life death — K-strategists with high somatic investment), Type II (constant mortality across age classes), and Type III (high juvenile mortality, low adult mortality — r-strategists with low somatic investment). In the world physics, the position on this continuum emerges from the agent's kappa — the heritable soma-vs-reproduction allocation fraction. The wear/repair mechanic must produce a continuous gradient of lifespans, not a binary outcome.

## Flows

Eight flows move energy and nutrient between stocks. Each flow is a rate — resources per tick — and each is subject to constraints described below. Energy and nutrient travel together through most flows (an agent that eats another agent acquires both energy and nutrient), but they have different fates: energy is progressively dissipated to heat, while nutrient cycles back through consumption of carcasses. Reserve is the central clearing account for energy: all income enters as reserve, all costs are paid from reserve, and surplus reserve can be converted to structure through growth.

### Input flows

**1. Photosynthesis.** Solar → Living agent (reserve). The only way energy enters the living system. Producers absorb energy from the constant solar flux into their reserve. This flow is attenuated by local competition — co-located producers share the available flux, weighted by both autotrophy investment and structure (body size). Bigger producers shade smaller ones, creating a growth feedback loop: an established producer that has accumulated structure captures a larger share of light, grows faster, and shades newcomers further. Any agent with autotrophy investment can photosynthesize — there is no gate. The producer/consumer divide emerges from two structural constraints — and per-trait maintenance cost is not one of them (it is breadth-neutral; see the cost structure section): the **nutrient bottleneck** (photosynthesis produces energy but not nutrient; an agent that photosynthesizes must also acquire nutrient via uptake from the available pool, which requires sustained substrate contact) and the **sessile–mobile incompatibility** (in the domain, movement breaks the sustained substrate contact uptake needs, so a roving hunter cannot also feed itself nutrient as an autotroph however much it invests; in this world it is economic for now, below). What divides a rooted producer from a mobile consumer is a conflict between their bodies, not the overhead of carrying both traits — an agent *can* combine autotrophy with the sessile form of heterotrophy (decomposition) and persist as a mixotroph. Photosynthesis moves energy only — nutrient must be acquired separately through nutrient uptake (flow 2). The incompatibility is **economic**: a moving autotroph pays per-distance movement cost for no extra light, and takes up nutrient as a still one does. Substrate contact, the domain's mechanism, is held in reserve for autotrophic uptake (flow 2). The mixotroph persists as a niche, not as the producers' dominant mode. Two signatures tell the niche apart from a route around decomposition, and they are **read and reported, not required**: the mixotroph's heterotrophy is **conditional**, falling where the pool under it is rich (the carnivorous-plant pattern; see *Capability and expression are decoupled*), and it drains a **minority** of the carcass pile, the rest going to heterotrophs by role. They are readouts of domain fidelity, read on every atlas as the heterotroph guild fractions are, and a failure of either does not by itself bring physics out of reserve. A physics change needs a failure of something the design selects for or stands on: robustness across initial conditions and its causes of death ([genesis search](genesis-search.md), *Genesis selects for worlds that are sensible across initial conditions*), the sensible-world criteria, conservation, or what the game needs. A signature can corroborate such a failure and point at its mechanism. It is not a target. The reason is what a target does to the physics. Genesis selects on neither signature, so a world shows them or not by what its other dynamics happen to produce. Treated as requirements, they pulled lever after lever out of reserve to make that chance come out right: the flow-3 deficit rule and the cross-trait term for conditionality, with carcass access waiting for the share, and neither signature was delivered. A pattern the search does not select for and the physics is repeatedly tuned toward is a pattern being imposed, which is the opposite of emergence. If the world drifts toward mixotrophs doing all the decomposing, which the domain says does not happen ([trophic roles](../ecology/trophic-roles.md), *Why decomposition falls to specialists*), the readouts show it, and it is argued as a fidelity failure on the evidence then. How they are read: the minority share on the settled half of a run, not the whole, since the founding transient, when founders die into a world with no heterotrophs yet, is not the world the share describes. A failed share is told apart by the absolute drains, not the ratio. **Mixotroph excess** means mixotrophs drain more of the pile, taking it from decomposers. A **decomposer deficit** means decomposers drain less, while mixotrophs hold steady or also fall. A deficit worsens the nutrient lockup the detrital pathway exists to prevent, so it is the one with consequences for robustness. Conditionality is read within worlds, as Spearman ρ of producers' effective heterotrophy against the pool nutrient under them on the settled half. It is reported as shown only where three things hold, decoded and at `founder_aggregation = 0` alike: the median per-config ρ is negative; the share of configs with ρ < 0 differs from a coin's (two-sided binomial p < 0.05); and a majority of the atlas's configs grouped into lineage clusters (8 Ward clusters on unit coordinates, since cells descend from a few emitter lineages and are not independent) have a negative median. A negative median alone is no relation: a world split as a coin would, with its median a hair below zero, shows nothing, and a pooled ρ ranks worlds at different nutrient levels against each other rather than reading any one of them. Grounded in terrestrial plant photosynthesis. Real producers capture a small fraction of incident solar radiation (1–2% of PAR; Monteith 1972, Zhu et al. 2010), and roughly half of gross primary production is consumed by the producer's own respiration (Gifford 2003). The remainder — net primary production — is the energy base for the rest of the ecosystem. In the world physics, solar flux is the external energy source and light share models spatial competition: an isolated producer receives full flux, a crowded producer receives a fraction weighted by autotrophy investment and accumulated structure. Larger producers shade smaller ones — a size-structured asymmetric competition consistent with real canopy dynamics (Weiner 1990). Photosynthesis produces energy but not nutrient — the producer must acquire nutrient separately through substrate uptake, which requires sustained contact. This couples energy capture to physical stillness: a mobile agent can photosynthesize but cannot access the nutrient needed to reproduce, making mobile photosynthesis a demographic dead end.

**2. Nutrient uptake.** Available pool → Living agent. Agents extract nutrient from the available pool at their location. The uptake rate depends on the agent's autotrophy investment — the same sessile infrastructure (analogous to roots) that captures light also extracts nutrients from the substrate. Co-located agents share the available pool proportionally, weighted by their effective uptake rate. Pool uptake is the autotroph's nutrient route (and, through hyphal uptake when it is on, a still heterotroph's, below); consumption (flow 3) is the heterotroph's — between them they are the only ways nutrient enters a living agent. Each tick's uptake is split immediately by **kappa**, mirroring the energy surplus split (flow 9): the (1 − kappa) share is earmarked as **reproductive nutrient** (off-limits to growth, reserved for reproduction), the kappa share added to the free store growth can bind. The same split is applied to nutrient acquired by consumption (flow 3) — the earmark is a life-history allocation, independent of acquisition route, so a consumer provisions offspring from ingested nutrient just as a producer does from absorbed nutrient. Unlike energy, nutrient has no retention buffer — there is no per-tick nutrient cost to hold reserve against, so the split applies to the whole acquired flow.

**Size-scaled uptake (latent, default off).** Light share scales with structure (flow 1); by default uptake does not, so a producer that grows gains light it cannot match with nutrient (#642). Uptake can instead grow with the body, as root mass grows with shoot mass and uptake capacity with root mass (Poorter et al. 2012; [plants](../ecology/plants.md), root:shoot): demand becomes `effective autotrophy × u_A × (structure / s_ref)^b`, with the **uptake structure exponent** `b` and the **uptake reference structure** `s_ref` world parameters. Structure is read where the light share reads it: uptake runs after photosynthesis and before growth, and neither moves it. Co-located agents still share a depleted cell in proportion to their (now size-scaled) demand, and at `b > 0` a body with no structure takes up nothing. `b` defaults to 0, where the size term is skipped, not evaluated, so demand is the size-blind rule bit for bit at every structure, and every existing recipe, pin and atlas world is unchanged, as with network redistribution (flow 5). `s_ref` defaults to 100: the body at which scaled demand equals size-blind demand, chosen so that switching `b` on leaves total producer demand of the same order. Over the atlas at `b = 0` (producers, ticks 1000–2000, autotrophy-weighted) the reference that leaves total demand unchanged is about 40 at `b = 1/3`, 85 at `b = 2/3` and 140 at `b = 1`; at 100 total demand moves by 0.74×, 0.90× and 1.40×. Producer structure is heavy-tailed (median 8, 90th percentile 460, founders near 2), so a reference at the typical individual would multiply total demand several-fold. Genesis searches `b` over `[0, 1]` and holds `s_ref` at 100. `b` changes the world's ecology, not only its numbers: switched on across the atlas it thins light-fed mixotrophy, turns producer heterotrophy down over the run, and trades nutrient lockup for monoculture, all monotone in `b`. Only a search with `b` in the box can find worlds that keep the lockup relief without the diversity collapse. The range keeps `b = 0`, so every current world stays reachable. Above 1 a large body would take up more per unit structure than a small one, which no domain evidence supports. `s_ref` stays out because at fixed `b` it moves results little and would cost the search a dimension ([645-size-scaled-uptake.md](../research/645-size-scaled-uptake.md)). The default of `b` stays 0 until a search with it in the box shows where worlds settle.

**Hyphal uptake (latent, default off).** In the domain, pool nutrient is not the autotroph's alone. Decomposers feed absorptively: they secrete enzymes into the substrate and absorb what is released ([trophic roles](../ecology/trophic-roles.md)). The same absorptive surface takes up mineral nutrient around it. Decomposers on nutrient-poor litter immobilise nutrient from the environment ([nutrient cycling](../ecology/nutrient-cycling.md)), and mycorrhizal hyphae take it up across far more substrate than roots reach and hand it to their host ([fungi](../ecology/fungi.md)). Animals, which ingest, do not. The world has no absorptive/ingestive split: heterotrophy is one trait, and the decomposer is a role, not a trait ([trait space](trait-space.md)). So stillness stands for the absorptive habit. Hyphal uptake rides on effective heterotrophy, gated by **substrate contact** (below), and pool demand becomes `(effective autotrophy + contact × effective heterotrophy) × u_A × (structure / s_ref)^b`. Roots and hyphae are one absorptive surface. They share a depleted cell in proportion to demand, as roots already do, because below-ground competition is size-symmetric among absorbing surfaces ([plants](../ecology/plants.md)). Giving roots priority instead would make the order of two calls a rule with no domain basis; fungi often out-compete roots for nutrient. There is no separate hyphal rate anchor. Nothing measured sets one apart from `u_A`, and a second rate would be one more fixed constant chosen arbitrarily. Contact is what keeps the flow off roving hunters. Without it every predator would draw on the pool, and the flow would not make the agent it exists to make, one poor in energy and rich in nutrient (*The partnership needs a partner*, flow 5). A still mixotroph absorbs through both sources, as a mycorrhizal plant out-absorbs a bare one. A new heritable absorptive trait would let ingestive-sessile and absorptive-mobile agents exist, but it adds an axis to the trait vector and re-encodes every atlas, for a distinction the domain ties to stillness anyway. Tying uptake to carcass feeding instead (immobilisation during decomposition) would give nothing between carcass pulses, and mineral uptake without a carcass is what defines the mycorrhizal partner. The flow is a world switch, off by default, under which demand is the autotrophic rule bit for bit, so every existing recipe, pin and atlas world is unchanged. It rides on heterotrophy that committed worlds already carry, so switching it on changes those worlds. It is read on the committed atlas by a rule fixed before the run (#654), and becomes physics, with a fresh search under it, only if that reading finds the partner. The partner is found when free nutrient per unit reserve is higher in heterotrophs by role than in producers by role in most worlds that carry both, and more often than with the switch off, without a loss of robustness across initial conditions.

In the world physics this incompatibility is **economic**. Every agent with mobility moves its full stride each tick, so the two coincide today, but contact written on movement follows behaviour if movement ever becomes conditional. It reads this tick's movement only, with no memory of past ticks. Movement is the tick's last repositioning phase and runs after uptake, so this tick's movement, as uptake sees it, is the most recent move phase: the stride that placed the agent in the cell it now absorbs from. ### Flows between living agents

**3. Consumption.** Agent (structure) → Living agent (reserve). A consumer eats a target's body through sustained physical contact, draining the target's structure. A consumer reaches a target within its **feeding reach** — `effective_heterotrophy × (contact_range_coefficient + body_reach_coefficient × √structure)`. Feeding reach, like reproductive reach (flow 4), has two physical solutions because feeding can close the gap to food two ways: move the organism (the contact-range term, the mobile solution) or extend the body to the food (the structure-derived body-extent term, the sessile solution — a decomposer's mycelium foraging by growing through the substrate). The whole reach is modulated by effective heterotrophy, so the foraging body is the *heterotrophic* body: an autotroph's bulk earns it no feeding reach, while a growing mycelium's does. The square-root form keeps the body term sublinear (a disc's radius scales with the square root of its area), so reach grows with body size without running away. This mirrors light competition (flow 1), where structure already makes a producer's body spatially consequential — body size matters spatially on both the autotrophic (shading) and heterotrophic (feeding reach) axes. The target can be a living agent or a carcass — the physics are identical, governed by the same heterotrophy trait. A carcass is an inert agent: it has structure, nutrient, a position, and a trait vector, but it does not act. Decomposition is consumption where the target happens to be dead — it requires no separate capability. The drained structure enters the consumer's reserve, lossy — the fraction retained depends on the trait-space distance between consumer and target (flow 7); the remainder dissipates to heat. The nutrient **bound in the structure removed** transfers alongside it (proportional to structure drained) — a living target's free store and reproductive-nutrient earmark are not touched, staying with it until death. The consumer retains only what it needs according to its stoichiometric demand, and excretes the excess immediately to the available pool at the consumption site (consumer-driven recycling: the non-limiting nutrient is released). What it needs from a bite is the nutrient that matches the bite's own energy **plus whatever its existing surplus energy is still waiting on**: `retained = min(released, ratio × energy gained + deficit)`, with `deficit = max(0, ratio × growth_efficiency × (reserve − buffer) − free nutrient)`. `ratio` is the consumer's stoichiometric demand per unit structure, `reserve − buffer` is the surplus the grow phase can mobilise (above the growth retention buffer, flow 9), and `growth_efficiency × (reserve − buffer)` is the structure that surplus could build. So the deficit is the nutrient the agent's own energy cannot be bound without, and it is zero for an agent whose free store already covers its surplus. Both terms are read at tick start, before any drain, like the rest of a consumer's demand. The kappa split below then divides what is retained between body and offspring. The cap reads the consumer's ratio and its current budget, never its size: two consumers with the same surplus and store that gain the same energy keep the same nutrient, whatever their bodies. This is the domain's rule. In consumer-driven recycling a consumer retains an element while it limits growth and releases it once it is in excess, judged against the consumer's **whole** budget (Sterner & Elser 2002; [animals](../ecology/animals.md)). For a heterotroph the food is its only carbon, so the budget is the bite: it is energy-limited, its deficit is about zero, and it keeps `ratio × energy gained`. A light-fed producer's carbon comes mostly from light. Where the pool under it is poor, its surplus waits on nutrient, the deficit is large, and it keeps the carcass nutrient that surplus needs, as a carnivorous plant keeps nearly all its prey's nitrogen. Where the pool is rich, uptake fills its store, the deficit is zero, and a carcass bite is worth to it only what it is worth to a heterotroph. This is what makes a drain's nutrient value **conditional on shortage**, and why the carnivorous-plant pattern can arise from stoichiometry without a gate (*Capability and expression are decoupled*). *Measured (#668, 2026-10-05), provisional, on the committed atlas, which was searched before this rule: light-fed mixotrophs keep 7–8× as much carcass nutrient as under `ratio × energy gained` alone, about 1 unit per unit of carcass structure drained against 0.14–0.18. But what a bite keeps is unrelated to the pool under the drainer: per-bite ρ against pool N is +0.02 pooled and per config decoded, and −0.03 pooled and +0.07 per config at `founder_aggregation = 0`. On these worlds the deficit makes drains pay everywhere, not only on poor ground ([668-cross-trait-calibration.md](../research/668-cross-trait-calibration.md)). Measured (#670, 2026-10-05), on atlases searched under this rule with `c_AH` searched: still unrelated. The median per-config ρ is −0.08 to +0.05, and 42–49 of 87–96 configs are negative ([670-cross-trait-verdict.md](../research/670-cross-trait-verdict.md)).* A cap of `ratio × energy gained` alone would treat a light-fed producer as if its only carbon were the bite, so a drain could never relieve its nutrient shortage beyond the bite's own energy, and its value would not depend on the pool. A cap scaled by the consumer's whole-body demand would let a large body keep most of a nutrient-rich bite and make a small one excrete nearly all of it, a size advantage the domain does not have. The consequence for carcasses is deliberate. A carcass carries its dead agent's whole free store and earmark, so it is far richer in nutrient per unit structure than any body ratio. Whatever a drainer does not need is mineralised to the cell, which is what decomposition does in the domain. It also makes the released nutrient a **public good**: whoever takes up from that cell gains it, not only the agent that drained the carcass, as with a decomposer's extracellular enzyme products ([trophic roles](../ecology/trophic-roles.md), *Why decomposition falls to specialists*). The deficit is a per-tick budget, not a per-bite allowance: a consumer reads it once at tick start, and what each bite keeps beyond its own `ratio × energy gained` is spent from it, so over a tick a consumer keeps at most its deficit beyond the energy-matched nutrient of all its bites, living and carcass alike. The deficit is the nutrient its surplus is waiting on, and that surplus waits on it once, however many bites the consumer takes. Bites draw on it in the drain pass's target-id order, never slice order. The retained nutrient is then split by **kappa**, exactly as pool uptake is (flow 2): the kappa share joins the free store growth can bind, the (1 − kappa) share is earmarked as reproductive nutrient. This is the heterotroph's route to a reproductive-nutrient earmark — ingested nutrient funds offspring, mirroring how absorbed nutrient does for autotrophs, and mirroring how consumption energy already reaches the reproductive allocation through the grow-phase split (flow 9). The limiting currency (energy or nutrient) constrains how much of the consumed material the consumer can actually use. Consumption of a living target is non-lethal by default; the target survives unless its structure drops below its complexity-dependent death threshold or its reserve reaches zero.

Grounded in animal consumption of living tissue and saprotrophic decomposition. Real consumers face two distinct modes: partial consumption (grazing, browsing) removes tissue but leaves the target alive and capable of regrowth, while lethal consumption removes the target from the living system entirely, creating a carcass (McNaughton 1979; Begon et al. 2006). Both modes operate through the same physical act — the consumer eats the target's body — and both are governed by the same heterotrophic capability. The ecological consequences diverge based on how much structure is removed relative to what the target can survive. In the world physics, this distinction emerges rather than being prescribed: consumption drains target structure, and the target's complexity-dependent death threshold determines whether the interaction is survivable. Whether the target is alive or dead does not change the trait required — heterotrophy applies to all consumption. Trophic transfer efficiency is governed by trait-space distance between consumer and target — consuming a biochemically similar agent yields more usable energy and nutrient than consuming a dissimilar one. In terrestrial ecosystems, direct consumption of living tissue accounts for only 5–15% of net primary production (Cyr & Pace 1993); the majority of energy reaches consumers through the detrital pathway — consumption of carcasses. The world physics should not assume consumption of living targets is the primary energy pathway for all consumer strategies. Real decomposition is dominated by fungi and bacteria (Wilson & Wolkovich 2011). Substrate quality controls decomposition rate: the lignin-to-nitrogen ratio of dead material determines how quickly decomposers can process it (Melillo, Aber & Muratore 1982). In the world physics, trait-space distance between consumer and carcass governs efficiency — a decomposer biochemically similar to its target extracts more usable energy and nutrient. Grounding the body-extent term: in real sessile decomposers the mycelium *is* the foraging organ — a transport and foraging network that grows through the substrate to reach resources (Fricker et al. 2017), so for a fungus growing bigger literally means reaching farther for food. The structure-derived term in feeding reach captures this: the body is the reach.

**4. Reproduction.** Living agent (reserve) → Living agent. Parents invest reserve and nutrient to create offspring. A reproductive event requires **both** earmarks to have accumulated past their thresholds: reproductive allocation ≥ reproduction energy threshold *and* reproductive nutrient ≥ reproduction nutrient threshold. (There is no longer a body-support nutrient gate — embodiment binds the body's nutrient by construction, so the only nutrient question at reproduction is whether enough has been earmarked to provision offspring.) The energy transfer is lossy — a fraction dissipates to heat (flow 8). Both parents survive. Reproduction has two modes, variable fecundity, and spatial dispersal of offspring.

The energy a parent invests is drawn from its **reproductive allocation** — the earmarked sub-account of reserve filled tick over tick by kappa's flow allocation in the grow phase (see flow 9). Reproduction does not draw from unallocated reserve. This decouples reproductive timing from current-tick reserve fluctuations: an agent whose unallocated reserve has just been depleted by metabolism can still reproduce if it has built up enough allocation, and an agent flush with unallocated reserve cannot reproduce until kappa has routed surplus into the allocation. The reproductive allocation is a labelled fraction of reserve — it is part of the agent's reserve for conservation purposes, but it is not available to fund metabolism, growth, or repair.

Nutrient is provisioned to offspring in exact parallel. Alongside the energy, the parent transfers **reproductive nutrient** drawn from its nutrient earmark — the (1 − kappa) share of nutrient income accumulated over many ticks, from pool uptake (flow 2) or consumption (flow 3) — and divides it among offspring the same way the energy is divided. Because both currencies are split by the realised (Poisson) offspring count, a high-fecundity event under-provisions each offspring in both energy *and* nutrient: an offspring born with structure but near-zero free nutrient cannot grow (its growth is nutrient-co-limited) and is selected against, so over-fecundity under nutrient scarcity self-corrects.

Dispersal also draws on the same reproductive energy budget, but **at the event rather than per tick**. Before the budget is divided among offspring, a fraction of it is spent building the propagule structures that carry offspring away from the parent — spores, plumes, fruit, buoyant husks. That fraction rises **superlinearly** with the dispersal trait (`coefficient × dispersal^exponent`, clamped to the budget), so broadcasting widely is disproportionately expensive (anti-generalist economics). The spent energy dissipates to heat (flow 8) — it does not vanish, and conservation holds: `committed investment = offspring reserve + offspring structure + dissipated`. Higher dispersal therefore leaves less to provision each offspring, the dispersal analogue of fecundity's quantity/quality trade-off. This is deliberately **not** a standing per-tick maintenance cost — asexual propensity remains the only reproduction trait that costs every tick (see trade-off #8); dispersal and fecundity express their trade-offs only when the agent reproduces, where selection always sees them. The propagule cost is keyed on the reproducing agent's dispersal in an asexual event, and on the parents' average dispersal in a sexual event (the same average that sets the offspring-scatter kernel).

**Sexual reproduction** is the primary mode. Two compatible agents within reproductive range each invest energy. Offspring traits are derived from both parents through crossover (each trait dimension drawn randomly from one parent) with heritable mutation. Compatibility is determined by trait-space distance — agents with similar traits are more likely to mate. Reproductive isolation is an emergent property of trait divergence, not a separate trait. There is no mate selectivity parameter; divergent lineages become reproductively isolated because their trait vectors are distant.

**Reproductive reach** is the spatial distance over which two agents can find each other to mate, and it has two sources because mate-finding has two physical solutions: move the organism, or move its gametes. A mobile agent finds mates through its mobility-derived perception — the same reach by which it perceives the surface (see Channels). A sessile agent cannot travel to a partner, so it broadcasts gametes instead: the same dispersal investment that scatters its offspring also carries its gametes to stationary partners (pollen, spores, broadcast spawning). An agent's reproductive reach is therefore derived from **both** mobility and dispersal, added smoothly — neither term gates the other, and there is no floor or ceiling. A wolf (high mobility, negligible dispersal) finds mates by moving; a producer (mobility 0, high dispersal) finds them by gamete broadcast; an agent with neither is reproductively isolated and can reproduce only asexually. This makes dispersal a dual-purpose trait — outbound it places offspring, inbound it reaches mates — and reach is asymmetric-friendly: a pair can form when *either* agent's reach spans the gap, so a wide-broadcasting producer can fertilise a near-sessile neighbour. Reach is the **spatial** axis of mating; reproductive compatibility distance (above) is the orthogonal **trait-space** axis. A sexual event requires both: partners close enough in space to meet, and close enough in trait space to be compatible.

**Asexual reproduction** is governed by an agent's asexual propensity trait — it is not a universal fallback. Each tick, an agent with sufficient energy and nutrient rolls against its asexual propensity: the trait sets the per-tick probability of reproducing alone, independent of whether compatible mates are nearby. A lineage that has lost asexual capability (low asexual propensity) cannot reproduce alone even when isolated; a lineage with high asexual propensity reproduces alone at the corresponding rate even when surrounded by compatible mates. Offspring traits are the parent's traits plus mutation — no crossover, because there is no second parent. The costs of asexual reproduction are inherent: lower offspring variation (no recombination) and a single parent's energy contribution (less total investment per offspring). Whether a lineage relies primarily on sexual or asexual reproduction is not prescribed — it is an emergent outcome of selection on asexual propensity under the population density and variation pressures the lineage experiences (see trade-off #8).

**Fecundity** — the number of offspring per reproductive event — is a heritable trait. A fixed total energy budget is invested per event; fecundity determines how many offspring share that budget. High fecundity produces many poorly-provisioned offspring (r-strategy). Low fecundity produces few well-provisioned offspring (K-strategy). The actual offspring count for a given event is stochastic — drawn from a Poisson distribution with mean equal to the fecundity trait. This stochasticity means reproductive failure (zero offspring despite energy investment) is possible. The energy cost of reproduction is committed before the outcome is determined — failed reproduction is costly, as it is in nature.

For sexual reproduction, the effective fecundity is the average of the two parents' fecundity traits. Because fecundity is part of the trait vector, it contributes to trait-space distance and therefore to mate compatibility — agents with very different reproductive strategies are less likely to mate. This creates reproductive isolation along the r/K axis without any special mechanism.

**Offspring dispersal** follows a Gaussian kernel centred on the parent's position. Most offspring land nearby; a tail of long-distance dispersers enables colonisation of distant habitat. The dispersal radius is determined by the parent's dispersal trait — a heritable life history dimension representing investment in dispersal structures and mechanisms (spores, seeds, fruits, explosive pods). Higher dispersal investment produces a wider kernel. Each offspring in a clutch is placed independently, so siblings from the same event scatter across the dispersal range.

Grounded in life history allocation theory. Real organisms face a fundamental budget problem: energy and nutrient invested in offspring is unavailable for self-maintenance, growth, or future reproduction (Williams 1966). The core axes of variation are offspring number vs. quality (Smith & Fretwell 1974), current vs. future reproduction, dispersal distance vs. offspring provisioning, and the mode of reproduction itself — sexual (recombination generates variation, but requires mate-finding) vs. asexual (no mate cost, but offspring are less variable). Sessile organisms must disperse gametes or propagules through the environment; mobile organisms can seek mates directly but pay locomotion and predation-risk costs to do so. In the world physics, reproduction is an energy and nutrient transfer from parent to offspring, lossy like all transfers. Fecundity determines how a fixed investment is divided among offspring — many fragile or few robust. Dispersal determines how far from the parent those offspring land — a separate axis of investment, independent of fecundity. Sexual reproduction requires trait-space proximity (compatibility) and physical proximity, coupling reproductive success to both ecological similarity and spatial context. Nutrient limitation blocks reproduction even when energy is abundant, reflecting the stoichiometric constraint that real organisms face: building offspring requires specific material ratios, not just calories.

**5. Network redistribution.** Living agent ↔ Living agent. Energy and nutrient move between living agents along **network connections** — a cooperative, bidirectional transfer, distinct from consumption (which is adversarial and one-way). It is the mechanism by which **mutualism becomes possible**. The energy component is lossy like every transfer (flow 7); nutrient is conserved, as nutrient always is. The mechanism has three parts: how a connection forms, how it persists, and what flows along it.

**Formation is trait-free and surplus-gated.** There is no network trait — the trait vector stays closed (a network dimension would contradict the deferred-dimension discipline, and making the capability a trait would *prescribe* which agents build the network, the opposite of what the domain wants: "which agents build network infrastructure is emergent, not prescribed"). Instead *any* agent may build a connection, and the discipline is the connection's **own energy cost**. An agent invests in a new connection only when it carries **reserve surplus** — reserve above its growth-retention buffer, the same surplus the grow phase mobilises — and pays a **creation cost** from it. This grounds "who builds the network" in economic standing (large, well-established agents become hubs; marginal agents cannot afford to build) without a trait and without prescribing a morphology. A connection can only be *initiated* between two agents within their existing **surface-contact** reach — the per-agent physical reach consumption and reproduction already use, so no separate network-build range is introduced: an agent can only wire up to a neighbour it has touched. Once built, the connection **persists independent of surface distance** — the wire outlasts the touch, so networked agents may drift apart on the surface and stay connected.

**Persistence selects.** Each connection carries a per-tick **maintenance cost**, paid by its builder (building is **unilateral**, mycorrhiza-style — the partner consents to and pays for nothing). Selection acts here, not at formation: an agent stressed for reserve sheds the connections it can no longer fund, and a connection dissolves immediately if either endpoint dies. This is what makes the mutualism **emergent** rather than declared — connections that pay for themselves persist; the rest evaporate. Selection is deliberately *blunt*: a stressed agent sheds connections without ranking them by per-partner value. A market-style refinement that preferentially keeps the highest-yielding partners (the Kiers reciprocity below) is a future deepening, warranted only if blunt selection proves to admit free-riders the persistence economics cannot shed.

**Flow follows the gradient, per currency.** Each tick, along each connection, **each currency independently flows from the agent that has more to the agent that has less**, at a bounded **redistribution rate** — a fraction of the gradient, never the whole difference in one step, the same bounded-rate discipline reserve mobilisation uses. The energy transfer pays a **flat cooperative transfer efficiency**: lossy (flow 7) but *distance-independent*, unlike consumption. Consumption's trait-distance efficiency (`base · exp(−decay·d)`) models the cost of breaking down biochemically unfamiliar **structure**; network redistribution moves **reserve** — fungible metabolic fuel, with no foreign body to break down — so the structural-distance penalty does not apply. Applying it would also tax the most valuable mutualism the hardest: a producer and a decomposer sit far apart in trait space, and a distance penalty would strangle exactly the exchange the network exists to enable. Nutrient moves free-store to free-store, conserved, no heat loss.

**Excreted surplus is routed to paying partners.** When a connected agent mineralises nutrient it cannot use, the surplus flow 3 excretes from a drain of a living target or a carcass, that nutrient goes to its connected partners' free stores instead of to the cell. Each partner's share is in proportion to the energy that partner delivered to it in the most recent redistribution. A partner that delivered none gets none. If no partner delivered energy, the surplus is mineralised to the cell exactly as for an unconnected agent. This is the nutrient leg of the trade, and it rides a *flux*, not a store. A heterotroph is homeostatic: it keeps the nutrient its stoichiometry needs and excretes the rest the same tick (flow 3; Sterner & Elser 2002), so its free store holds no surplus for the gradient flow above to move. Where it coexists with producers it is limited by carbon, not by nutrient (Daufresne & Loreau 2001; [stability and resilience](../ecology/stability-and-resilience.md)). What it has to trade is the nutrient it releases from what it eats. That is the domain's ectomycorrhizal and ericoid exchange. Those fungi decompose organic matter and hand the nutrient to their host across the root interface, not into bulk soil, in exchange for carbon (Read & Perez-Moreno 2003; [fungi](../ecology/fungi.md), *The producer's route to organic nutrient*).
- *Why the split follows payment:* it is Kiers et al.'s (2011) reciprocal reward on the partner's side, more nutrient to partners that deliver more carbon. It closes the free-rider opening an equal split leaves, without a price.
- *Why not split by need:* that would route nutrient to whoever lacks it regardless of payment, which is charity, not a market.
- *Why the payment stays the energy gradient flow:* the producer's carbon flows first, unconditionally. A rule that also scaled the energy leg by nutrient received would deadlock a new connection, with no carbon meaning no nutrient meaning no carbon.

The producer's own sanction stays the builder's persistence decision (below). Routing changes nothing for an unconnected agent, and nothing at all while the network is off.

Drains run before redistribution within a tick, so the split reads the previous tick's energy legs, which `World` keeps (`last_partner_payments`). **Mutualism and the rejection of parasitism both fall out of these two gradient flows plus the persistence economics — no explicit market is needed.** A producer (limited by nutrient, holding energy) connected to a decomposer (limited by energy, releasing nutrient) sends energy down the energy gradient and receives the decomposer's routed surplus in return: each gains the currency it lacks, the connection improves both balances, and it persists — the carbon-for-nutrient trade of Kiers et al. (2011), *emergent* from gradient flow rather than priced by a market rule. A would-be free-rider that is poor in *both* currencies has nothing to send back up either gradient; it only drains the builder, whose balance never improves, so under any stress that connection is the first to go. Reciprocity is enforced by the builder's persistence decision, not by partner-pricing or sanctions.

Grounded in mycorrhizal network exchange. Real ecosystems contain transport infrastructure built by organisms — primarily fungal hyphae and root systems — that move resources between producers (Simard 2018). This is not passive diffusion; it is a market-like exchange governed by local supply-demand ratios (Kiers et al. 2011): plants allocate more carbon to fungal partners that deliver more nutrient, and fungi more nutrient to plants that deliver more carbon. The network transforms point-to-point competition into a connected system where resources flow along supply-demand gradients, hub nodes (large, well-established agents) disproportionately shape system-wide distribution, and the whole acts as a buffer against local resource shocks — damping oscillations by redistributing from surplus to deficit.

**Default-disabled.** The mechanism's knobs — creation cost, maintenance cost, the per-agent **connection cap** (the topology limit that keeps the network from collapsing into a fully-connected blob and bounds the per-tick cost), redistribution rate, and transfer efficiency — are world parameters, but they **default to off**: a zero connection cap means no connection ever forms, so Flow 5 is inert. This mirrors the no-op defaults of `body_reach_coefficient` (0.0) and `reserve_mobilisation_rate` (1.0) — every committed recipe, the current atlas, and the genesis search stay bit-unchanged until the network is deliberately enabled. There is **no genesis criterion that selects for mutualism**: the network is a latent capability the physics makes *possible*, exercised in seeded scenarios and available for a future epic to switch on and measure — not a pattern genesis currently hunts for. The redistribution pass is fully deterministic (surplus-gated formation, gradient flow), so it adds no new RNG; when the connection cap binds, candidate partners are taken **farthest first in trait space**, ties broken by partner agent id. The literature's mycorrhizal connection joins partners of different kingdoms; plant–plant links in a common network run only through a fungus (Simard 2018). Distance ranking prefers dissimilar partners smoothly, with no gate, role reading or knob. It never forbids a link, so with no dissimilar neighbour in reach producers still connect to producers, but kin and same-kind neighbours, which sit close in trait space, lose priority when the cap binds. A gate on complementarity (one endpoint heterotrophy-dominant, or gradients running both ways) would be a cliff in the traits, and since a homeostatic decomposer's store gradient is about zero, it would reject the very pair the network exists for.

### Flows from living to dead

**6. Death.** Living agent → Carcass. When a living agent dies, its remaining structure and nutrient become a carcass at the same location on the surface. The carcass's energy content is the agent's structure at the moment of death. The nutrient content reflects the nutrient the agent had incorporated.

Death has two triggers — either is sufficient:

- **Reserve depletion.** Reserve reaches zero. The agent can no longer fund metabolism. This is starvation (metabolic costs exceed income) or the terminal consequence of predation draining a target that was already energy-marginal.
- **Structural depletion.** Structure drops below a complexity-dependent threshold. The threshold scales with trait spread: an agent whose investment is concentrated in few dimensions (low complexity) tolerates greater structural damage — like a moss that functions the same at half its mass. An agent whose investment is spread across many dimensions (high complexity) is fragile — specialised organs and systems cannot sustain partial loss. The threshold is derived from the trait vector, not a separate parameter.

Death also has two distal causes. **Extrinsic mortality** — consumption drains structure below the threshold, or competition/shading makes the agent energy-negative until reserve depletes. **Intrinsic mortality** — somatic wear degrades functional traits until the agent can no longer acquire enough energy to cover its metabolic costs, then reserve slowly depletes. In practice these compound: an aging agent with degraded autotrophy that was previously viable becomes energy-negative when a competitor shades it, or an aging consumer whose effective heterotrophy has declined can no longer catch sufficient prey. Somatic wear makes death inevitable on a long enough timeline — even in the absence of predation or competition, an agent whose kappa is too low to keep pace with wear accumulation will eventually degrade to the point of energy bankruptcy.

Grounded in mortality ecology across all kingdoms. Real organisms die from two broad causes: extrinsic mortality (predation, disease, physical disturbance) and intrinsic mortality (senescence — the progressive failure of somatic maintenance; Kirkwood 1977). These causes compound: an aging organism with degraded function that was previously viable becomes fatally vulnerable when conditions shift (Ricklefs 2008). Death transfers embodied energy and nutrient from the living stock to the dead stock — creating the carcass pool that funds decomposition. What the organism accumulated over its lifetime determines the value of the carcass: a long-lived, well-fed agent leaves an energy-rich carcass; a starved juvenile leaves an energy-poor one. In the world physics, death has two triggers — reserve depletion (starvation) and structural depletion below a complexity-dependent threshold — reflecting the distinction between metabolic failure and structural collapse. Both routes produce a carcass that retains the dead agent's structure, nutrient, and trait vector.

### Dissipation

**7. Trophic transfer loss.** Accompanies flows 3, 4, 5, and 9. At every energy conversion — between agents or between reserve and structure within an agent — a fraction is lost to heat. This is not a separate event — it is the inefficiency inherent in every conversion. It is what makes trophic pyramids inevitable: each level of transfer dissipates energy, so less is available at each successive level. The path from target structure → consumer reserve → consumer structure involves two lossy conversions, compounding the loss. Nutrient is not lost to heat — it is either incorporated into the receiving agent or returned to the available pool.

The fraction lost varies with the relationship between consumer and target. Agents that occupy similar positions in trait space are biochemically similar — their structure is built from compatible machinery. Consuming a biochemically similar target is inherently more efficient: less energy is wasted breaking down unfamiliar structures, more of the target's embodied energy and nutrient is usable. Consuming a biochemically dissimilar target is less efficient: more energy is lost in conversion. Trait-space distance between consumer and target determines trophic transfer efficiency, by an exponential decay in that distance:

```
efficiency = base_trophic_efficiency · exp(−trophic_distance_decay · d)
```

where `d` is the Euclidean distance between the consumer's and target's trait vectors. `base_trophic_efficiency` is the efficiency at zero distance — the ceiling, when consumer and target are biochemically identical — and `trophic_distance_decay ≥ 0` sets how sharply efficiency falls as the target grows dissimilar (at decay 0 efficiency is flat at the base, independent of distance). Both are searchable world parameters. The exponential form keeps efficiency in `(0, base]` for all distances — it never goes negative or exceeds the base — so the dissipation of flow 7 is bounded but never fully escaped. This produces the ecological pattern without a special rule: consumers eating other consumers (small trait distance) capture a high fraction of energy and nutrient per unit consumed, while consumers eating producers (large trait distance) capture a low fraction. The trophic chain's increasing per-interaction efficiency at higher levels — and the corresponding decrease in available biomass — emerges from trait-space geometry.

Grounded in Lindeman's (1942) trophic-dynamic concept. Real trophic transfer efficiency ranges from 5–20% between trophic levels (Welch 1968), decomposed into assimilation efficiency (fraction of consumed energy absorbed) and net production efficiency (fraction of assimilated energy converted to biomass). Assimilation efficiency varies systematically with diet similarity: carnivores assimilate 60–90% of ingested energy because animal tissue is chemically similar to consumer tissue, while herbivores assimilate 15–80% depending on food type (Begon et al. 2006). Net production efficiency varies with thermal strategy: ectotherms convert 25–75% of assimilated energy to biomass, while endotherms rarely exceed 5% due to thermoregulatory costs. In the world physics, trophic transfer efficiency is derived from trait-space distance between consumer and target, producing the ecological pattern without a special rule: consumers eating biochemically similar agents retain a higher fraction than consumers eating dissimilar agents.

**8. Metabolism.** Living agent (reserve) → Heat. Every living agent pays a continuous energy cost from reserve simply to exist. This cost has three components:
- A base rate — the minimum cost of being alive, independent of traits or activity.
- Trait maintenance costs — each capability (autotrophy, heterotrophy, mobility) costs energy to maintain whether or not it is currently in use. These costs scale superlinearly with each trait's *own* investment, which makes **each trait self-limiting**: beyond some investment the accelerating maintenance overtakes the marginal income, capping how far a specialist pushes a single capability. Note what this does *not* do — because the costs are independent and additive, convexity penalises *concentration*, not *breadth*: spreading a fixed investment across two traits costs no more (and under a convex curve, slightly less) than piling it into one, so per-trait maintenance alone does not favour specialists over generalists. The forces that actually penalise breadth are functional incompatibility and structural fragility, set out in the cost structure below. One pair is also charged jointly: the autotrophy × heterotrophy cross-trait cost (trade-off #5) adds a term in the product of the two traits, zero for a specialist in either. Sensing is not separately priced — it is subordinate to mobility, and the cost of sensing range is folded into mobility maintenance (a mobile agent that reaches further also perceives further).
- Somatic maintenance — governed by kappa, the fraction of surplus energy allocated to soma vs. reproduction. Higher somatic allocation slows aging but competes directly with the energy available for reproduction and growth. This is the mechanism behind the reproduce-vs-survive trade-off.

Metabolism dissipates reserve to heat. It does not release nutrient — nutrient leaves a living agent only as the bound portion of grazed structure (flow 3) or, in full, at death (flow 6). This makes decomposition structurally necessary for nutrient cycling.

Grounded in metabolic theory and DEB theory. Real organisms pay a continuous energy cost to exist — basal metabolic rate scales with body mass as M^(3/4) (Kleiber 1932; West, Brown & Enquist 1997). Metabolic cost has multiple components: basal maintenance, activity costs (locomotion at 10–15× basal rate during active movement; Schmidt-Nielsen 1972), and the cost of maintaining functional machinery whether or not it is in use. DEB theory (Kooijman 2010) formalises maintenance priority: when reserves are insufficient, maintenance takes precedence over growth and reproduction — the organism cannibalises its own capacity to stay alive. In the world physics, metabolism is the sum of base rate and superlinear trait maintenance costs. Each capability costs energy to maintain regardless of use, and the cost accelerates with that capability's *own* investment level — making each trait self-limiting. This per-trait convexity is breadth-neutral; the specialist–generalist differentiation is produced not here but by functional incompatibility between capabilities and by structural fragility (cost structure, below).

**9. Growth.** Living agent (reserve) → Living agent (structure). When an agent's reserve exceeds a retention buffer sized to cover the next tick's metabolic costs, the excess is **mobilised at a bounded rate**, and the mobilised flow is split by **kappa**, the agent's heritable soma-vs-reproduction allocation trait. Two parameters govern this. The retention buffer is `metabolic_cost × growth_retention_multiplier` (a searchable world parameter, default 2.0); only reserve above this buffer is mobilisable. Of that mobilisable excess, a fraction set by the **reserve mobilisation rate** (a searchable world parameter, default 1.0) is mobilised this tick — `mobilised = reserve_mobilisation_rate × (reserve − buffer)` — and the remainder stays in reserve to be mobilised on later ticks. This is DEB-style flow allocation realised through DEB's **energy conductance**: reserve is drawn down as a *rate* — a bounded fraction of standing reserve per tick — and kappa partitions that flow at the moment it is mobilised, never the *stock* of reserve in a single step. The kappa fraction of the mobilised flow is committed to soma (spent that tick on somatic repair and on growth — converting reserve to structure, lossy, with a fraction dissipating to heat per flow 7). The (1 − kappa) fraction is committed to the agent's **reproductive allocation** — an earmarked sub-account of reserve that accumulates across ticks until a reproductive event fires (flow 4).

The mobilisation rate is what makes reserve do its job as a **feast-famine buffer**. The asymmetry between producers and consumers makes this load-bearing: a producer's photosynthetic income arrives every tick, so its reserve is continuously refilled regardless of how fast it is drawn down; a consumer's income arrives in discrete meals, so between meals it must live off standing reserve. At rate 1.0 the entire above-buffer excess is mobilised in a single tick (`mobilised = reserve − buffer`), which is the degenerate case the rate generalises away from — it works for producers but defeats reserve's buffering role for any agent with intermittent income. A tempting simpler design — mobilise the whole above-buffer excess every tick — is deliberately **not** the committed default behaviour, because it liquidates an agent's entire standing reserve on its first well-fed tick. A freshly seeded or newborn agent is provisioned with a substantial reserve (see the offspring structure fraction below); under one-tick liquidation that provisioning is converted to reproductive earmark, a little structure, and a great deal of conversion heat on tick one, leaving the agent at the bare retention buffer with no cushion. A consumer so stripped starves before it can establish a feeding income — not because it is non-viable, but because the mechanism gutted its provisioning before it could be tested. The bounded rate spreads mobilisation over many ticks (DEB's energy conductance: a large reserve mobilises a fraction per tick, never all at once), so the provisioning persists as a survival buffer and a within-life windfall — a large meal — is stored in reserve and drawn down gradually rather than dumped and largely dissipated. The rate is searched by genesis; the default 1.0 reproduces the unbuffered one-tick behaviour exactly, so it is a no-op against existing recipes, and search moves to rate < 1 where buffering produces more viable consumer ecologies.

This is an **energy-only** mechanism. The nutrient free store has no parallel mobilisation rate because, unlike reserve, it funds no per-tick cost — there is nothing to buffer it against (flow 2). Nutrient is earmarked at acquisition (the kappa split applies to the whole acquired flow), so a standing free-nutrient store is never liquidated into the reproductive-nutrient earmark; only reserve, which both funds metabolism and feeds the reproductive allocation by stock drawdown, needs the rate.

Growth also **binds nutrient**: structure is matter, so building it consumes free nutrient from the agent's store and locks it into the body at the stoichiometric demand (`bound nutrient += built structure × demand`). Growth is therefore co-limited (Liebig's law of the minimum) — the structure built this tick is the smaller of what the soma energy affords and what the free (unearmarked) nutrient store supports; energy that cannot be matched with nutrient stays in reserve rather than burning. Growth can only ever draw the *unearmarked* free nutrient — the reproductive-nutrient earmark is off-limits — so however greedily a well-fed agent grows, it cannot consume the nutrient reserved for reproduction. The bound nutrient stays locked in the body until the structure is grazed (flow 3) or the agent dies (flow 6); only then does it re-enter circulation, which is why decomposition is structurally necessary to free the nutrient that growth sequesters. Growth itself is not a decision — it is an automatic consequence of being well-fed. Newborn agents are not born structure-zero: the **offspring structure fraction** (a searchable world parameter, default 0.2) sets what share of each offspring's per-offspring energy budget is committed to structure rather than reserve at birth. The structure commitment passes through the same lossy reserve-to-structure conversion as in-life growth (`growth_efficiency`), with the unconverted remainder dissipated to heat — so conservation across reproduction reads: parents' committed investment = sum of offspring reserve + sum of offspring structure + heat (per-tick reproduction efficiency loss plus per-offspring growth-efficiency loss). The same provisioning is applied to seeded agents at world creation, so tick-0 agents start with structure consistent with their initial energy budget. Because the structural death threshold is a *fraction of peak structure* (flow #9) and a newborn's or seed's birth structure *is* its peak high-water mark, every agent is born above its own threshold regardless of size — small is not the same as dying, so no seed or birth viability gate is needed. After birth, structure accumulates by the kappa-allocated growth flow described above, advancing the peak the threshold is measured against. The growth rate depends on the soma share of available surplus after metabolism is paid.

Allocating at the flow rather than at the stock has three consequences. First, the energy committed to reproduction is protected from being consumed by later metabolic costs — once earmarked, it stays earmarked until a reproductive event draws it down. Second, an agent's reproductive timing depends on the *integral* of its surplus history, not on the current tick's reserve balance. A long-lived agent with intermittent surplus can still reproduce by accumulating allocation over many ticks. Third — the consequence the mobilisation rate adds — standing reserve buffers the agent against intermittent income: because only a bounded fraction is mobilised per tick, a reserve cushion persists across lean ticks instead of being immediately committed away, which is what lets a consumer survive the gaps between meals. The total energy in the agent — unallocated reserve + reproductive allocation + structure — is what conservation tracks; the partition between unallocated reserve and reproductive allocation is internal bookkeeping.

Grounded in DEB theory's reserve-to-structure conversion. Real organisms grow by converting metabolic surplus into embodied biomass — a lossy process constrained by the balance between assimilation and maintenance. DEB theory predicts that growth rate decelerates as body size increases, because maintenance costs scale with existing structure while assimilation scales with surface area — producing the von Bertalanffy growth curve as an emergent property (Kooijman 2010). Growth is not a decision but an automatic consequence of surplus: a well-fed organism grows, a starving organism does not. Structure once built is durable but not free — it must be maintained, creating a feedback where larger agents have higher maintenance costs and need more income to sustain themselves. In the world physics, growth converts reserve surplus to structure after metabolism and reproduction costs are paid. The conversion is lossy, with a fraction dissipated to heat. Newborns are provisioned with a non-zero share of structure at birth via the same lossy conversion (see the offspring structure fraction above), reflecting that real organisms hatch or germinate as already-embodied tissue rather than as disembodied reserve.

### Flow summary

Energy flows one way: source → living system → heat. All energy income enters as reserve. Reserve funds metabolism, growth, and reproduction. Growth converts reserve to structure (the body). Consumption drains structure (from living targets and carcasses alike) into the consumer's reserve. Nutrient cycles: available pool → living agents (free store, then bound into structure by growth) → released by grazing or death → carcasses → (via consumption of carcasses) → available pool. Metabolism releases reserve to heat but never nutrient; nutrient leaves a living agent only as the bound portion of grazed structure or, at death, in full.

```
Solar ──photosynthesis──▶ Reserve ──growth──▶ Structure ──death──▶ Carcass
  (source)                  │                    ▲                    │
                        metabolism           consumed by          consumed by
                        reproduction         another agent        another agent
                            │                    │                    │
                            ▼                    ▼                    ▼
                          Heat            Consumer's reserve   Consumer's reserve
                          Offspring        (with trophic loss)  (with trophic loss)
                                                 │
                                            ▲    │
                  Available pool ◀───────── │ ───┘
                       │              nutrient excretion
                  nutrient uptake     (stoichiometric mismatch,
                       │               consumption of carcasses)

```

### Unit anchors (trait-to-flow conversions)

Traits are dimensionless ([trait-space](trait-space.md)), but five committed forms read a trait *as* a flow or a length. Each conversion carries a **unit anchor** — a constant that turns one trait unit into so much of a base unit per tick — which the rules previously left implicit. They are now named, with the tick's status as a committed unit choice, and each is `1.0` in today's units:

| conversion | rule | anchor | units | code constant |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| distance moved per tick `= u_M · effective mobility` | movement (channels on the surface) | `u_M = 1` | `L/T` per trait unit | `units::MOBILITY_DISTANCE_PER_TICK` |
| offspring placement kernel `Normal(0, σ)`, `σ = u_D · dispersal` of the seed parent | flow 4, both modes | `u_D = 1` | `L` per trait unit | `units::DISPERSAL_KERNEL_SIGMA` |
| nutrient uptake demand per tick `= u_A · effective autotrophy` (`× (structure / s_ref)^b` when size-scaled uptake is on) | flow 2 | `u_A = 1` | `N/T` per trait unit | `units::AUTOTROPHY_NUTRIENT_UPTAKE_PER_TICK` |
| structure drained per in-reach target per tick `= u_H · effective heterotrophy` | flow 3 | `u_H = 1` | `E/T` per trait unit | `units::HETEROTROPHY_STRUCTURE_DRAIN_PER_TICK` |
| base repair per functional trait per tick `= u_R · kappa`, attenuated by `exp(−repair_decay · wear)` | somatic wear, flow 9 | `u_R = 1` | `E/T` per unit kappa | `units::KAPPA_REPAIR_PER_TICK` |

One length anchor reads movement rather than a trait. The **contact distance** `d_c = 0.1 u_M` (`L`) scales substrate contact, `exp(−d / d_c)` for distance `d` moved this tick (flow 2). It is fixed in units of the mobility anchor, so it moves with `u_M` if that is ever rescaled. In code it is the world parameter `contact_distance`, defaulting to `DEFAULT_CONTACT_DISTANCE`; genesis does not search it.

A second family of anchors sits inside use-dependent wear (*Somatic wear*, above). `use_wear_rate` is one coefficient applied to three usages of different dimension; the constant that makes each product homogeneous is likewise `1.0` today:

| coupling | rule | anchor | units | code constant |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| autotrophy wear per tick `= use_wear_rate · u_WA · energy captured` | somatic wear | `u_WA = 1` | `E/E` (dimensionless) | `units::USE_WEAR_PER_ENERGY_CAPTURED` |
| heterotrophy wear per tick `= use_wear_rate · u_WH · energy drained` | somatic wear | `u_WH = 1` | `E/E` (dimensionless) | `units::USE_WEAR_PER_ENERGY_DRAINED` |
| mobility wear per tick `= use_wear_rate · u_WM · distance moved` | somatic wear | `u_WM = 1` | `E/L` | `units::USE_WEAR_PER_DISTANCE_MOVED` |

With `u_WA` and `u_WH` dimensionless, `use_wear_rate` is itself a pure number for the two energy couplings, and `u_WM` alone carries the `E/L` that the mobility coupling needs. The use-wear branch is guarded bit-exactly on a configuration where all three couplings are live at once (`tests/trait_unit_anchors.rs`).

Naming them changes no number and no trajectory (`crates/explorers-sim/src/units.rs`; guarded bit-exactly by `tests/trait_unit_anchors.rs`). What it changes is what the design claims: the trait unit is pinned simultaneously to `L/T`, `L`, `N/T` and `E/T`, so no rescaling of energy, length or nutrient is a symmetry of the map even when every explicit parameter scales along — three of the search box's 32 axes are physical only through these anchors (see [viability](viability.md), *Dimensionless groups*, and [`440-dimensionless-groups.md`](../research/440-dimensionless-groups.md), smell S1).

**One anchor sits outside the stepper.** The energy-death prefilter ([viability](viability.md), *Gate — energy death*) charges nutrient for "one minimal viable body" of structure `S_min`. The committed death threshold is peak-relative (`fragility × peak_structure`, *Structural death* above) and commits no absolute floor, so the rules give `S_min` no value; the prefilter substitutes `STRUCTURE_MIN = 1 E` (`explorers_search::prefilter::STRUCTURE_MIN` — a search-crate constant, not a world rule, so it is not in `units.rs`). Naming it changes no verdict; it is recorded in viability's Open tier and left for the designer (#461, [`440-dimensionless-groups.md`](../research/440-dimensionless-groups.md), smell S3).

**Trait-space distance mixes these anchors.** The Euclidean distance `d` that drives trophic transfer efficiency (flow 7), mate compatibility (flow 4) and the branching descriptor is computed on the *raw* trait vector. It therefore adds a length-like coordinate (mobility, dispersal) to an energy-rate-like one (heterotrophy), a nutrient-rate-like one (autotrophy) and pure fractions (kappa, asexual propensity, fecundity) as if they shared a unit — which they do only because every anchor is `1`. This is stated, not fixed: whether the anchors should become world parameters (and, if so, what `d` then means) is a design decision to be taken with the designer, not a change any of the committed rules make.

## Viability constraints

The flows above describe what can happen. Viability constraints describe what the flows must be capable of producing for the ecology to function. These are not parameter values — they are design requirements on the physics. If any constraint cannot be satisfied by some agent configuration under the maintenance cost landscape, that trophic role is structurally impossible and the ecology is broken.

The constraints here are stated qualitatively. Their *a priori* counterpart — closed-form conditions on the world parameters that decide when a constraint cannot be met for *any* agent configuration — is developed in [viability.md](viability.md), which analyses when these requirements are satisfiable before any simulation is run.

### Individual balance

Every agent has two currencies — energy and nutrient — each with its own income and cost structure per tick.

**Energy income** enters reserve through acquisition flows: photosynthesis (flow 1) or consumption (flow 3, which covers both living targets and carcasses). Each flow's yield depends on the agent's trait investment (autotrophy or heterotrophy), the availability of the resource it targets, and amplifying factors like accumulated structure.

**Energy cost** leaves reserve through metabolism (flow 8): base rate, trait maintenance, and somatic maintenance (governed by kappa). Cost is determined by the agent's trait vector and activity.

**Nutrient income** enters the agent through uptake from the available pool (flow 2) or alongside energy during consumption (flow 3). Uptake requires autotrophy investment — the same sessile infrastructure that captures light. Consumption transfers nutrient directly from the target (living or carcass), requiring no autotrophy. Either route's income is split by kappa at acquisition — the (1 − kappa) share to the reproductive-nutrient earmark, the kappa share to the free store — so reproduction is fundable from whichever route an agent's trophic strategy uses.

**Nutrient cost** is driven by reproduction. Nutrient limitation blocks reproduction but does not impair other functions. An agent that is nutrient-negative cannot replace itself even if it is energy-positive.

An agent is **viable** when it can achieve positive balance in both currencies over the timescale needed to grow, reproduce, and replace itself. Energy viability without nutrient viability produces agents that survive but cannot reproduce — a demographic dead end. Nutrient viability without energy viability is impossible, since the agent starves before it can reproduce.

### Trophic viability

Each trophic role imposes specific viability requirements across both currencies.

**Producers.** Photosynthetic income must exceed producer metabolic cost. The difference — net primary production — is the energy available to the rest of the ecosystem. If producers barely break even, nothing else can live. Producers acquire nutrient through uptake from the available pool, driven by autotrophy investment. A sessile producer-specialist invests heavily in autotrophy, which covers both light capture and nutrient extraction. The physics must allow such an agent to generate substantial energy surplus while acquiring enough nutrient to reproduce.

**Consumers.** Consumer energy income — whether from grazing living agents or from consuming carcasses — must exceed consumer metabolic cost. Consumer income is downstream of net primary production: consumers can only extract what producers make available as structure. Consumers acquire nutrient alongside energy through consumption — no autotrophy needed. But the nutrient content of prey depends on the prey's own nutrient accumulation — consumers inherit whatever nutrient-to-energy ratio their targets carry. A consumer whose stoichiometric demand exceeds what prey provides is nutrient-limited despite successful feeding.

**Decomposers.** Decomposer energy income from processing carcasses must exceed decomposer metabolic cost. Decomposer income is downstream of mortality: decomposers can only extract what death makes available. Decomposers also acquire nutrient from carcasses, and excrete excess to the available pool — closing the nutrient cycle. A world without viable decomposers locks nutrient in carcasses indefinitely, eventually starving producers of nutrient even while solar flux continues to provide energy.

## Topologies

The world has two topologies. Each topology is a geometry on which agents interact. Different flows operate on different topologies.

### Physical surface

A two-dimensional continuous surface. Movement happens here. Every agent and every carcass has a position on this surface.

The surface is the topology for all spatially-local interactions. Photosynthesis, consumption, nutrient uptake, death, and reproduction all require surface proximity. Sensing, chemotaxis, and light competition also operate on the surface.

### Emergent network

A graph that agents build through their behaviour. Nodes are agents; edges are connections that agents create and maintain. The network is not constrained by surface distance — two agents far apart on the surface can be adjacent on the network if they have built a connection between them.

Network-building is a general, **trait-free** capability: any agent can invest reserve to create a connection — there is no network trait (see flow 5). What disciplines it is the connection's own cost set against what flows along it, so only some agents sustain connections: those carrying the **reserve surplus** to build, and whose surplus/deficit profile a connection actually improves. In real ecosystems this role is filled by organisms with specific morphology — fungal hyphae, root systems — but here which agents build network infrastructure is **emergent, not prescribed**, falling out of the economics rather than a trait or a morphology gate. A connection is initiated only between agents within **surface-contact** reach, then persists independent of surface distance once built.

The network enables flows and perception that bypass surface locality. Its topology, density, and reach are all emergent properties; the formation, persistence, and exchange rules live with flow 5.

## Channels

A channel defines what can flow between agents — information, energy, or both — and on which topology. An agent's trait vector determines the range at which it can interact on each channel. Two agents are in **contact** on a channel when they are within the range that their traits allow on that channel's topology.

### Channel types

Channels are distinguished by whether they can carry energy.

**Perception channels** carry information only. They tell an agent what is nearby and in what direction, enabling decision-making, but they do not move energy or nutrient. Looking at food does not feed you; detecting a chemical signal does not transfer resources.

**Physical channels** carry energy and nutrient between agents. They are the pathways through which trophic flows operate. Consumption, reproduction, nutrient uptake, and network redistribution all require a physical channel.

This distinction is a law of physics, not a strategy choice. No amount of trait investment can make a perception channel carry energy. Energy transfer always requires a physical channel — either physical contact on the surface or a connection on the network.

### Channels on the surface

The surface carries both perception and physical channels. Each operates at a range determined by the interacting agents' traits.

- **Surface perception** — detecting agents and carcasses on the surface. Chemical gradients, visual detection, vibration. The range at which an agent perceives its surroundings is derived from its mobility investment — sensing is subordinate to mobility, not a separately-priced capability. A more mobile agent both reaches further and perceives further. Signals are distance-weighted: closer agents produce stronger signals. Perception range bounds the interactions an agent initiates by sensing; the one exception is mate-finding, where gamete broadcast (dispersal) extends a low-mobility agent's reproductive reach beyond what it could perceive — a sessile producer finds mates it cannot sense (see Reproduction, reproductive reach).
- **Surface contact** — physical interaction on the surface. Required for consumption, reproduction, and nutrient uptake. The range at which an agent can physically interact is determined by its trait vector — not a uniform world parameter. An agent's physical reach is a property of the agent, not the world.

### Channels on the network

The network carries both perception and physical channels.

- **Network perception** — signals propagated through network connections. Chemical alerts, resource-state information. An agent connected to the network can perceive the states of connected agents regardless of surface distance.
- **Network transfer** — resource movement through network connections. Required for network redistribution (flow 5). This is what makes the network more than a signalling system — it is infrastructure that moves energy and nutrient.

### Consumption is incremental, and its character emerges from target and size

Consumption is not instantaneous, but neither does it run on a duration clock. A consumer drains a target's structure tick by tick for as long as the target stays within its feeding reach (flow 3). "Over time" is just repetition: reach is binary each tick (in range or not), and the cumulative effect is the sum of the per-tick drains while contact persists. The per-tick drain is the consumer's effective heterotrophy scaled by its hunger (below) — capped by the structure actually present and shared proportionally among co-feeders — with no warm-up, ramp, or reward for staying put.

The three heterotrophic interactions ecology names — predation, grazing, scavenging — are not separate mechanisms or traits but one capability differentiated by **target state** and **the size of the drain relative to the target** ([trophic roles](../ecology/trophic-roles.md), *Body size and the geometry of heterotrophic interactions*):

- **Predation** — the per-tick drain is large relative to the target's structure, so the target crosses its complexity-dependent death threshold (trade-off #9) within a tick or a few: lethal, and the consumer takes essentially the whole body. The wolf-and-hare event.
- **Grazing** — the drain is small relative to the target's structure, leaving it above the death threshold: non-lethal, the target survives and regrows, and the consumer exploits a renewable flow across repeated visits. The caterpillar-and-tree relationship; the 5–15% of net primary production the grazer chain carries.
- **Scavenging / decomposition** — the target is a carcass; the same drain physics apply to dead structure.

All three fall out of mechanisms already committed — the death threshold (#9) decides lethality, trait-space distance decides transfer efficiency (flow 7) — so the lethal/non-lethal divide is emergent, not prescribed, and one heterotrophy trait spans all three exactly as the domain requires.

The sessile-versus-mobile feeding trade-off lives in **feeding reach** (flow 3), not in any contact duration. Feeding has two physical solutions: a mobile consumer reaches food by locomotion (the contact-range term) and pays movement cost to do so; a sessile consumer reaches food by growing its body through the substrate (the body-extent term — a decomposer's mycelium is its foraging organ). A roving predator and a rooted fungus feed by the same per-tick drain but close the gap to food in opposite ways — the wolf runs, the mycelium grows.

A tempting alternative — making feeding effectiveness ramp with contact *duration*, so staying put feeds better — is deliberately **not** used, and not merely as a calibration choice. It corresponds to no variable in the domain: ecology says predation-versus-grazing is a size-ratio phenomenon, not a duration one. And any duration ramp keyed to stillness forbids mobile feeding outright — a chemotactic pursuer that moves to reach prey resets its own clock every tick and never eats, defeating the very pursuit predators the sensing and chemotaxis machinery exists to serve. The autotroph's stillness requirement (trade-off #2) is expressed through the cost structure and through reach, never through a temporal gate; the same holds for heterotrophy.

#### Capability and expression are decoupled: expression is ungated

Heterotrophy is **capability**: the most an agent can drain per tick from each target in its reach. What it drains is **expression**, that capability scaled by a factor in [0, 1]. Capability and expression stay decoupled, so a regulated intake can be added without a new trait. But expression is **ungated**: it is 1 for every consumer in every state, and what an agent drains is its capability, shared among co-feeders and reduced toward resembling living targets by recognition (next). Light-fed mixotrophs' kin-killing is left to recognition, dispersal and the cross-trait cost (trade-off #5), not to their state of hunger.

Expression is ungated because no gate has an outcome it can be held to. Every form tried had one job: sate the light-fed mixotroph, so that its heterotrophy mostly idled, while leaving the consumer hungry enough to produce. The forms were reserve held, surplus above the buffer, intake this tick against a ceiling, and fullness (intake averaged over a clearance time). Each failed that job, for a reason that belongs to the worlds, not to the form: the two populations' intakes overlap, so no reading of the consumer's own state ranks them apart. The record follows. That job now belongs to recognition, dispersal and the cross-trait cost. What remains for expression is fidelity alone. In the domain a consumer's intake is regulated, not fixed: Holling's functional response saturates with handling time, and satiation lowers feeding. A **regulated intake** of that kind is the faithful form, and it is **latent**: there is no measured failure it would correct, and its defaults would have no outcome to bound them. The design's rule is that a default is bounded by the outcome it stands for, not by a proxy and not by eye (see the bounds below). A gate tuned without an outcome would bring back what the surplus gate did, a cap on consumers' production that nothing asked for. Regulated intake comes out of latency when a measured failure names its outcome, for example consumers overgrazing a renewable prey to collapse at rates the domain does not show.

The ungated drain was already the reading of #656, #668 and #670, which read worlds searched under the surplus gate with expression set to 1. The surplus-above-buffer gate is removed, not only its default: #624 found it fails consumers, whose earmark fill as heterotrophs by diet was 18–39× less than ungated ([624-surplus-gate-measurement.md](../research/624-surplus-gate-measurement.md)). A stock gate is a form this section shows to be wrong, so it is not kept as a latent dial: turned up it would bring back a known cap on consumers' production. Regulated intake, if it comes, comes as a new form with its own outcome. Heterotrophy is **capability**: the most an agent can drain per tick from each target in its reach. What it actually drains is **expression**: capability scaled by a smooth, decreasing function of the consumer's **satiation**. Satiation is how **full** the consumer is. Fullness is its recent intake per tick from every route (light as well as what it drains), accumulated and cleared over a few ticks, and read against its **intake ceiling**. A consumer well below its ceiling feeds at full capability. One near its ceiling feeds at about half capability, and one well above it scarcely feeds at all. The gate is co-limited across energy and nutrient, so hunger follows the emptier currency. The response is smooth throughout, with no threshold (the smooth-landscape constraint below). Its parameters are world physics tuned by genesis, not traits.

The form has three parts.

- **Fullness.** An agent carries an energy fullness `F_E`, a moving average of its energy intake per tick: `F_E ← F_E + (intake_E − F_E) / τ`. Intake is photosynthesis plus the energy it received from drains. The **clearance time** `τ` (ticks) is a world parameter. The nutrient fullness `F_N` is the same average of nutrient intake, which is pool uptake plus the nutrient it retained from drains.
- **Ceiling.** The **intake ceiling** is `C = k × metabolic_cost` per tick, where `k` is a world parameter. The nutrient ceiling is the nutrient that would match it in growth, `C × growth_efficiency × ratio`, where `ratio` is the stoichiometric demand per unit structure.
- **Expression.** Each currency's expression is `1 / (1 + (F / C)^4)` against its own ceiling, and the consumer expresses the emptier one, `E = max(E_E, E_N)`. The expressed drain toward each target is `capability × E`. Where growth binds no nutrient (`growth_efficiency = 0`), the nutrient side has no ceiling and the gate reads energy alone.

The exponent is fixed at 4 because the gate has to act almost like a threshold without being one. A consumer at half its ceiling keeps 94 % of its drain, and one at twice its ceiling keeps 6 %. A softer response bleeds production from consumers well short of their ceiling, which is how the earlier forms failed, while a steeper one gains little and approaches a cliff. A third parameter would mostly trade off against `k`. Each currency's term is smooth, so the drain has no step. It has a kink only where the emptier currency switches.

Expression is read once per consumer, at the start of drain resolution, from the fullness carried into the tick. One read before any feeding means a meal from a living target does not sate the consumer further before the carcass pass, and the expression is identical toward every target. Co-feeders split a scarce target in proportion to their *expressed* demand. Fullness is updated once per agent after drain resolution, from that tick's light, uptake and drain income. So a meal is taken whole on the tick it arrives, and it sates the consumer on the ticks that follow, for as long as its fullness takes to clear. A newborn inherits its parent's fullness in both currencies at birth, as it inherits provisioned reserve and nutrient (flow 9). A lineage fed by light starts sated, and a consumer lineage starts where its parent stood. A founder, present when the world begins and with no parent, starts at the intake its first tick shows before any drain: its light and its uptake. Photosynthesis and uptake run before the first read, so a light-fed founder starts sated and a consumer founder starts hungry, each as its own income says. A fixed starting value would instead read every founder as equally fed, whatever its income.

- **It is how consumption works in the domain.** Per-capita consumption is a behaviourally modulated flow, not a fixed rate (Holling's functional response — [animals](../ecology/animals.md)); hunger lowers the attack threshold and widens what is acceptable ([cannibalism and kin](../ecology/cannibalism-and-kin.md), *Drivers*). - **Satiation reads recent intake, not any reserve stock.** In the domain, a consumer is sated by what it has recently taken in, against what it can process, and that sits well above upkeep. Covering maintenance does not stop it eating, and its surplus is what it grows and breeds from ([animals](../ecology/animals.md), *Consumption as a regulated flow*). No reading of reserve can carry that, for two reasons that are properties of the grow phase, not of a particular yardstick:
  - **A stock gate caps a consumer's production.** The grow phase spends exactly the reserve above the retention buffer, so a gate that closes on that reserve closes on the quantity production is drawn from. Take a consumer whose only income is its drain, with income at full capability `I` and surplus `s` in ticks of maintenance, and mobilisation rate `m`. It settles where `I / (1 + c·s) = 1 + m·s`, so its production is held near `√(I / c·m)` rather than `I − 1`. The sensitivity only moves the cap.
  - **A stock gate ranks the light-fed mixotroph as the hungrier agent.** Light arrives as a thin steady flow that the grow phase spends every tick, while a consumer banks meals. So the mixotroph holds less reserve above its buffer than the consumer does, whatever capacity the reserve is divided by.

  *Measured: under the reserve-held gate (#605/#606/#619), killers held near the buffer and were rarely sated. Under the surplus-above-buffer gate (#622–#624), light-fed mixotrophs held a median surplus of 0.05–0.13 ticks against consumers' 0.8. At its default sensitivity, heterotrophs by diet filled their earmark 18–39× less than ungated ([624-surplus-gate-measurement.md](../research/624-surplus-gate-measurement.md)).*
- **It reads intake averaged over time, not this tick's intake.** Consumers live on feasts and famines. Most ticks they take in little, and their production comes from rare meals worth several to tens of ticks of maintenance. A light-fed mixotroph takes in a steady few ticks of maintenance from light every tick. So a consumer's feast tick looks like a mixotroph's ordinary tick, and any gate on this tick's intake, or on this tick's drain potential, either clips the feast or leaves the mixotroph room. Averaged over a few ticks, the order is clean: the mixotroph's light keeps it full, and the consumer, near break-even on average, stays below its ceiling. Reading before feeding and updating after lets a feast be taken whole. The meal then sates the consumer while it clears, which is what satiation does in the domain. *Measured (#629, #634): per-tick intake against a ceiling failed whether the ceiling scaled with body or with heterotrophy, and whether the saturation was soft or sharp (at best 26–33 % of killers sated with 41–45 % of production kept). Mixotroph kin killers average about 4× maintenance in intake, against 0.7–1.1× for heterotrophs by diet ([634-ceiling-region.md](../research/634-ceiling-region.md)).*
- **Light counts toward fullness.** An actual gut never holds light, so fullness is nutritional status rather than literal gut fill. A light-fed agent is well fed, and the gate has to see that, or its heterotrophy would fire whatever its state. This is the domain's pattern of a well-lit carnivorous plant forgoing prey.
- **The ceiling scales with the body alone.** A large, costly body processes more, so the ceiling is a multiple of maintenance. It does not scale with heterotrophy. Light-fed mixotroph killers are as heterotrophic as diet-fed consumers (median effective heterotrophy about 0.5–0.6 in both groups, #634), so a heterotrophy term would separate nothing, and would give the killers the higher ceilings. What separates the populations is the reading, not the ceiling.
- **A newborn inherits its parent's fullness,** because the gate matters most at birth. A newborn lands beside its parent and its siblings. If it started empty, a newborn mixotroph would read hungry for its first `τ` ticks and graze its siblings, at exactly the life stage the gate protects. Inheriting fullness follows the provisioning already in the physics: the parent hands down reserve and nutrient, and with them its fed state.
- **The clearance time is searched, not fixed.** It trades how strongly a fed consumer is damped against how much production the gate costs it. It does not affect whether a light-fed mixotroph is sated, since steady light keeps it full at any `τ`. Gut passage time varies widely across taxa in the domain.
- **Both outcomes bound the defaults at once, and directly.** A gate calibrated on one population can fail the other unseen, which is how the stock gate's sensitivity failed. A bound on a proxy can be met while the outcome fails, which is how the per-tick ceiling's percentile window failed (#629).
- **Satiation is not the reproductive allocation,** although a well-fed agent banks surplus there too. The allocation empties at every reproductive event, so a parent would read as most hungry on the tick after its newborn appears beside it — the moment kin-grazing does its damage. It measures where the agent is in its reproductive cycle, not how well fed it is.
- **Satiation is counted in ticks of maintenance,** so the same intake means "plenty" to a small, cheap body and "a little" to a large, expensive one. A raw-energy gate would make big consumers permanently sated and small ones permanently ravenous.
- **Satiation is co-limited, not energy-only,** because consumption delivers both currencies and an agent can be rich in one and starved of the other. A producer saturated with light but short of nutrient has a real reason to drain: the structure it removes carries bound nutrient. This is the carnivorous-plant pattern — well-lit producers that consume for nitrogen and phosphorus, and cut back when nutrient is plentiful ([trophic roles](../ecology/trophic-roles.md), *Trophic roles as network positions*). The clause's warrant is that **conditionality**: in the domain, carnivory is expressed where the limiting nutrient is short and wanes where it is added (*Sarracenia*; [trophic roles](../ecology/trophic-roles.md), *Mixotrophy*). It holds in the world only while producers' effective heterotrophy falls as the pool nutrient under them rises. A world where the most heterotrophic producers sit on the richest ground is not this pattern, and the clause does not justify it. Under the retention rule of flow 3 a light-fed drainer keeps only its ratio × the energy a bite gains it, so the nutrient pull of a carcass is mostly mineralisation into the drainer's own cell, which it shares with its neighbours. *Measured (#642–#656): conditionality ran backwards under the flow-3 retention defect. Under the corrected rule, on a fresh atlas with `b` searched, it no longer runs backwards but is not shown either: within worlds there is no measurable relation. The clause therefore describes the intended pattern, not the measured world. Its physics is the flow-3 deficit rule together with the autotrophy × heterotrophy cross-trait term (trade-off #5), from #662 ([655-retention-readouts.md](../research/655-retention-readouts.md), [656-fresh-atlas-verdict.md](../research/656-fresh-atlas-verdict.md)). Measured (#668, 2026-10-05), provisional: the deficit rule alone does not bring conditionality (median per-config ρ −0.017 decoded, +0.015 at `founder_aggregation = 0`), and what a light-fed drainer keeps from a carcass is unrelated to the pool under it ([668-cross-trait-calibration.md](../research/668-cross-trait-calibration.md)). Measured (#670, 2026-10-05), with the cost searched: not shown within worlds in three of four readings, and a world's `c_AH` does not predict its per-config ρ. Only seed 43 decoded shows a clear negative split ([670-cross-trait-verdict.md](../research/670-cross-trait-verdict.md)).* An energy-only gate would silence exactly the consumption that recycles a carcass pile when nutrient is locked up, the failure the detrital pathway exists to prevent. Growth is already co-limited by Liebig's law (flow 9); satiation reads the same two currencies the same way.
- **The gate is uniform across target state.** It reads the consumer, never the target, so a carcass and a living agent are drained under the same expression. Gating only living targets would hand carcasses a second advantage over living prey — on top of the one real asymmetry, that a carcass cannot move away (see [trait space](trait-space.md), *Decomposer is a behavioural role*) — and push the design toward a decomposer by construction. It is not needed to keep matter cycling: carcass drawdown matters where nutrient is locked up, and there nutrient satiation is low, so the gate is open. Where nutrient is plentiful the pile is not what limits the world. Decomposers in the domain are need-gated too — microbes raise nutrient-acquiring enzyme production when that nutrient is scarce ([trophic roles](../ecology/trophic-roles.md)).
- **It adds no heritable axis.** Expression is derived from state the agent carries (its fullness, and the metabolic cost and stoichiometric demand its traits and structure set), consistent with agents as automata whose behaviour follows from trait vector, spatial context and current state ([execution model](execution-model.md)). Fullness is new state, but it is state, not a trait.
- **Hunger still erodes restraint.** A starving consumer feeds at full capability on whatever is in reach that does not resemble it, and still feeds, at a reduced rate, on what does — the domain's "hunger → less discrimination" loop is preserved, not switched off. Need-gating reduces *gratuitous* consumption; sparing relatives is the separate job of recognition (next).

#### Recognition: living targets that resemble the consumer are spared

Expression is suppressed toward a **living** target in proportion to how closely it resembles the consumer in trait space. Suppression is strongest at zero trait distance and falls away smoothly over a **recognition distance**, a world parameter measured on the same trait-space metric as the reproductive compatibility distance. Carcasses are never spared.

The form subtracts from expression. A living target's **resemblance** to its consumer is `w(d) = (1 − (d/D)²)²` for trait distance `d` below the **recognition distance** `D`, and `0` at or beyond it: `1` toward an identical target, falling smoothly to `0` with zero slope at `D`. The consumer's expression toward that target is `max(0, E − r·w(d))`, where `E` is its expression (above, ungated, so 1) and `r = 1` is the **recognition restraint**, a fixed share of capability. Toward a carcass, or a living target at or beyond `D`, expression is `E` unchanged. So:

- An identical living target is spared completely, and a newborn one mutation step away nearly so (at `mutation_magnitude` 0.05 and `D = 0.5`, `w ≈ 0.98`, so it is drained at about 2 % of capability).
- The restraint fades smoothly with distance, to nothing at `D`. Kin beyond `D` are drained at full capability.
- The drain is continuous in trait distance, with zero slope at `D`.

`D` is a world parameter (default `0.5`). `D = 0` is the kin-blind limiting case. Nothing resembles its consumer there, and the drain is exactly capability. The default is half a trait unit, measured on the whole-vector metric. At `mutation_magnitude` 0.05 that is ten mutation steps from a parent, so a parent's offspring and a tight cluster fall inside it. *Measured (#606, under the previous reserve-based gate; #624 re-measures under the surplus gate): the search box ranges `mutation_magnitude` over [0.01, 0.5], so in searched worlds `D` is often one to a few steps. `sample:31` (σ = 0.31) puts `D` at 1.6σ, and 14 % of its births land beyond it; on the atlas 25 % do, and on the LHS draw 21 % (matched live configs) to 39 % (all configs). A quarter to a third of kin kills are of kin beyond `D` ([606-recognition.md](../research/606-recognition.md)). Under that gate, raising `D` helped only once `c` had pulled expression at the retention buffer below the restraint. At `D = 1.5`, the near-producer steps on `sample:31` also spare the producer ([619-scale-vs-structure.md](../research/619-scale-vs-structure.md)).* It is well short of the separation between a producer and a consumer guild (about 1.4 between `autotrophy ≈ 1, heterotrophy ≈ 0` and the reverse), so recognition does not shelter prey from a predator of another guild. It sits at a quarter of the default reproductive compatibility distance (2.0): a lineage spares a narrower circle than it can mate with. Genesis does not search `D`. Every world it searches runs at the default 0.5, because genesis selects on nothing kin-related, and an unselected dimension spreads by search geometry alone and only adds noise to the search (as `c_AH` did, #670, and `λ`, #686 and #711). A wide-mutation world therefore spares a smaller share of its offspring than a narrow one, by the shares the *Measured* note above gives. Whatever value `D` takes, it is bounded by that guild separation, not by mutation: past about 1.4, recognition begins to shelter prey from a predator of another guild (#619 saw `D = 1.5` shelter the producer from near-producer steps). `D` is an absolute trait distance rather than a multiple of `mutation_magnitude` for the same reason. A distance scaled by mutation size would cross the guild separation in wide-mutation worlds and need a cap to stop it, while the parent–offspring distance also depends on how often mutation fires, so a multiple of σ would not cover a fixed share of offspring anyway. The restraint `r` is 1, fixed, and not a world parameter. Recognition carries the whole job of sparing kin: expression is ungated, so no hunger state lowers `E` to help it, and with any `r < 1` a consumer would always drain its identical kin at `1 − r` of capability. At 1, near-clones are spared and the share spared falls smoothly with resemblance, so kin at moderate distance are still eaten in part. It is not searched because genesis selects on nothing kin-related, and an unselected dimension spreads by search geometry alone (as `c_AH` did, #670). Like `D`, it adds no search dimension.

Why this shape:

- **Without it, indiscriminate consumption lands on kin.** Offspring start life next to their parents and siblings, so in a viscous population a consumer that cannot tell kin from non-kin meets its relatives first ([cannibalism and kin](../ecology/cannibalism-and-kin.md), *Dispersal and kin competition*). A sessile parent's newborns sit inside its feeding reach, and their tiny structure turns a graze into predation. Kin grazing of newborns is then the ordinary mortality regime, and a lineage cannot climb from producer toward heterotroph without first evolving out of reach of its own young.
- **Pricing cannot do this job; only suppression or separation can.** Agents do not choose targets: everything inside feeding reach is drained. Lowering transfer efficiency at small trait distance (a pathogen-cost analogue) would lower the consumer's reward for eating kin, but the victim dies all the same. Only two levers change the victim's fate: the drain toward it is not expressed (recognition), or it is out of reach (dispersal). Dispersal is already a trait; recognition is what the physics lacks.
- **The transfer-efficiency kernel stays as it is.** It prices a near-identical target highest, and that is faithful: conspecific tissue is the best-matched food there is (flow 7). In the domain, what holds cannibalism in check is not poor nutrition but inclusive fitness, pathogens and injury. The kernel says kin are good food; recognition says kin are not eaten when the consumer can afford to refrain.
- **The cue is phenotype similarity, read off the whole trait vector.** That is how recognition works in the domain: tiger salamanders discriminate by smell, fungal colonies fuse only when identical at every self/non-self locus, and *Dictyostelium* segregates more strongly the more genetically distant the strains are ([cannibalism and kin](../ecology/cannibalism-and-kin.md), *How organisms avoid eating kin*; *Beyond animals*). The trait vector is the agent's phenotype, so it is its own recognition signature; no separate signature dimension is needed. The recognition distance parallels the reproductive compatibility distance — one physics threshold on trait distance decides who can mate, the other who is spared.
- **Similarity spares near-identical non-kin too, and that is accepted.** A similarity cue cannot tell a sibling from an unrelated member of the same tight cluster, so within-cluster predation happens mainly under hunger. *Measured (#606, under the previous reserve-based gate; #624 re-measures under the surplus gate): it does, but hunger was the ordinary state (see need-gating above). Under recognition, 99.7 % of kills of kin within trait distance 0.1 are by grazers under half expression. Within-cluster predation did not fall, and on `sample:31` the step lineages' newborns are grazed by kin about as often as before recognition ([606-recognition.md](../research/606-recognition.md)).* In the domain conspecifics are a small share of most diets, and recognition by cue shares the same limitation. Tracing pedigree instead would be exact but corresponds to no mechanism an organism has, and gives selection nothing to act on.
- **Living targets only,** because the reason to spare kin is the reproductive value that dies with them. A dead relative has none. Sparing carcasses would also block the path matter most needs in a lockup: a lineage recycling its own cluster's dead. This is a difference in what the target is *worth*, not in how it is drained, so it does not bring back a dead-versus-living split in the drain physics.
- **Hunger does not relax it, because there is no hunger to read.** In the domain starvation weakens kin discrimination without abolishing it (spadefoot tadpoles; the *hunger → less discrimination* loop). That relaxation is latent with regulated intake (above). The cost is that cannibalism of near-clones, with its population-level roles (density regulation, the life-boat mechanism, bistability), is left to kin beyond the recognition distance and to partial drains at moderate distance. If regulated intake returns, recognition's coupling to `E` is redesigned with it: at `r = 1`, `max(0, E − w)` spares an identical target fully even at `E = 1`, so hunger would have nothing to relax.
- **Age and timing are not the cue,** because a newborn refuge from every consumer would remove predation on the young generally — the most common form of cannibalism and predation in the domain — rather than kin predation specifically.

### Hard distance predicates are an accepted discontinuity in position

Several surface interactions are decided by a **hard distance predicate**: a pair is either inside a radius or outside it, with no soft edge. The radii are smooth in parameters and traits; membership is a step function of position. Three such predicates run in every world:

- **Feeding reach** (flow 3): a target within `consumption_reach` is drained, one beyond it is untouched.
- **Light-competition membership** (flow 1): a producer's shading weight joins a neighbour's light share only when the two lie strictly within `light_competition_radius`.
- **Mate-finding reach** (reproduction): a candidate beyond the seeker's reach (effective mobility × sensing coefficient + dispersal × dispersal-reach coefficient) is skipped.

Network formation adds a fourth when the network is enabled (a partner within `contact_range_coefficient` is eligible), and chemotactic sensing counts neighbours within the sensing radius. Feeding reach is the worked case below; the others are the same class and are accepted for the same reasons.

#### Feeding reach

Feeding reach is deliberately a **binary contact predicate**: each tick a target is either within the consumer's reach (distance ≤ `consumption_reach`) and drained, or beyond it and untouched. There is no soft edge. The reach *radius* is smooth in the consumer's traits and structure — `effective_heterotrophy × (contact_range_coefficient + body_reach_coefficient × √structure)` has no floor, ceiling, or threshold — but whether a given pair is in contact is a **step function of position**. A target at distance `reach − ε` is drained in full; one at `reach + ε` is not drained at all.

That step is reachable from ordinary arithmetic. The boundary itself is a measure-zero set, but f32 positions are rounded, and two computations of the same geometry that differ only in rounding — the same roster founded one whole nutrient cell over on the torus, say — can land a consumer–target pair on opposite sides of it. The toroidal-translation property (`crates/explorers-sim/tests/prop_symmetry.rs`) found exactly this once consumption entered its domain (#453, #477): roughly 8e-5 of cases diverged macroscopically after a drain, with diagnosed pairs at distance `2.078908` versus `2.078909` against a reach of `2.0789087`. In one world the target is drained, in the other it is not, and everything downstream forks. This is sensitive dependence on initial conditions, not an asymmetry in the physics; no f32 tolerance can absorb it.

#### Light-competition membership

The same thing happens at the light-competition radius (#548). A producer's light share is its weight (effective photosynthesis × structure) over the summed weight of every producer strictly within `light_competition_radius`, so a neighbour at `radius − ε` shrinks the share and one at `radius + ε` does not touch it. The translation property found a mobile world (founding photosynthesis centred on zero with wide trait covariance, so many founders were producers) where, at tick 6, producers 5 and 10 sat at distance `2.8233688` in one world and `2.8233724` in the other, against a radius of `2.8233697`: inside by 9.5e-7 in one, outside by 2.6e-6 in the other. One producer's reserve forked at tick 7 (12.66 against 7.87) and the rest of the trajectory followed. The share *fraction* is continuous in the weights; only neighbourhood membership steps.

#### Why these are accepted

They are accepted rather than smoothed for two reasons. First, the discontinuity is in **position**, not in any parameter the genesis search optimises. The search moves world parameters and founding traits; positions are state the stepper produces, and the search sees their effect only through aggregates over many pairs and many ticks, where a measure-zero boundary contributes no systematic cliff. The *Smooth parameter landscape* constraint (below) is about the response to *parameter* changes, and the radii — the parameter-facing part of each mechanism — are smooth. Second, any fix is new physics that touches every interaction near its edge: smoothing feeding reach would change who feeds on whom in every world, and smoothing the light kernel would change every producer's income, not only the rare boundary pairs. Each would need its own ecological grounding and re-validation of every calibrated regime.

The consequence is for guarantees, not for ecology. **Spatial symmetry properties — toroidal translation, and any other that relates two worlds by a rigid motion — hold only away from these boundaries.** Near them they are sensitive by construction. This is why the translation property test rejects any case in which, between ticks, a pair sits within `TRANSLATION_BOUNDARY_TOLERANCE` (4e-5, twice the worst-case accumulated position rounding) of a threshold the next step will apply (`pair_on_hard_boundary`): a consumer on its feeding reach to a target or carcass, two producers on the light-competition radius, or a possibly-eligible, trait-compatible pair on a seeker's mate-finding reach. About 0.8–0.9% of cases are rejected. Such a trajectory is outside the property's domain, not a counterexample to it. Any future spatial-symmetry property must exclude every hard predicate its domain reaches in the same way; network contact and chemotactic sensing are off in the translation domain and so are not guarded there.

The alternatives considered, so a later revisit starts from them (stated for feeding reach; each applies to the light-competition kernel in the same way):

- **Soft contact edge.** Replace the binary predicate with a kernel that ramps the drain from full to zero across a band around the reach radius (linear, or a smooth logistic in `distance − reach`). Outcomes become continuous in position and the symmetry guarantee sharpens to a tolerance. Cost: a new band-width parameter, a drain that depends on distance within the band (so partial contact becomes a thing the ecology must justify), and a change to every drain near the edge.
- **Grid-quantised contact.** Decide contact on quantised positions (for example, snapped to a fixed lattice or to nutrient-cell coordinates) so that translation by a whole cell maps contact sets exactly. This buys exact translation symmetry for whole-cell shifts but not for arbitrary ones, and it trades a measure-zero discontinuity for a coarse lattice one — reach becomes a staircase in distance, and contact depends on where the lattice falls.

Revisit if a boundary flip ever matters at the level the search observes — a regime whose outcome turns on a single drain-or-not decision — or if an exact spatial-symmetry guarantee becomes load-bearing.

## Cost structure (trade-offs)

The cost structure is what prevents any agent from being good at everything. It rests on smooth, convex forces, no one of which is a hard gate. **Superlinear per-trait maintenance** makes each capability self-limiting — its maintenance cost grows faster than linearly with that trait's investment, so there is a finite best investment in any single trait. On its own this caps extremity but is *breadth-neutral*: because the per-trait costs are independent and additive, spreading a fixed investment across many traits is no more expensive than concentrating it (and under a convex curve, slightly cheaper), so this mechanism alone does not favour specialists. What penalises breadth is two other forces: **functional incompatibility** between capabilities (most sharply, the substrate-anchored stillness autotrophy and nutrient uptake require versus the movement mobility and predation require — a physical conflict, not an accounting one) and **structural fragility** (broad trait investment spreads the body across interdependent systems that cannot sustain partial loss). Where genesis search finds these too weak — generalists dominating where they should be confined — the reserve lever is to commit a **cross-trait interaction maintenance term** (a cost in the *product* of co-invested traits, zero for a specialist and positive for any breadth), generalising the autotrophy–mobility steepening named in trade-off #2; it is held in reserve rather than committed standing so the landscape stays as smooth and as few-knobbed as possible. One pair, autotrophy × heterotrophy, is specified as a latent term (trade-off #5); it and every other pair stay in reserve. Together these produce nine fundamental trade-offs:

**1. Acquire vs. maintain.** Every capability costs energy to maintain whether or not it is currently in use. More capability means more overhead. Superlinear maintenance costs ensure that investment in any single trait is self-limiting — each additional unit of investment in that trait costs more to maintain than the last. This caps extremity *per trait*; on its own it does not penalise spreading investment across traits (see #5).

**2. Sessile vs. mobile.** Sessile and mobile strategies face fundamentally different resource access. The sessile strategy dominates for autotrophs because sunlight is ambient — mobility costs energy for zero additional energy income. A mobile autotroph pays locomotion costs but does not reach more light than a stationary one. In the domain the divide is physical: the sustained substrate contact that nutrient uptake requires is broken by movement. In this world it is economic: movement costs energy per distance and buys no light, and nothing else stops a mobile agent photosynthesizing or taking up nutrient. Per-trait maintenance does not reinforce this — paying for two traits independently is no dearer than paying for one (see #5). Substrate contact is the world's predicate for hyphal uptake (flow 2). Applying it to autotrophic uptake is held in reserve for a robustness or game failure that mobile autotrophy explains, and steepening the autotrophy–mobility cost *interaction* (a cross-trait term, not the per-trait costs) is second (flow 2).

**3. Reproduce vs. survive.** Energy invested in offspring is energy not available for self-maintenance. Energy allocated to somatic maintenance via kappa (slowing wear) is energy not available for reproduction. This is the disposable soma trade-off: build a new body or repair the current one. Both achieve the same goal — keeping a lineage alive — through different strategies. High kappa produces long-lived agents that reproduce infrequently. Low kappa produces short-lived agents that reproduce early and often. The optimal position on this continuum depends on the extrinsic mortality rate: when predation is high, investing in longevity is wasteful because the agent is likely to be eaten before aging matters.

**4. Few quality vs. many fragile offspring.** A fixed reproductive energy budget can produce few well-provisioned offspring (K-strategy) or many poorly-provisioned offspring (r-strategy). Fecundity determines how many offspring share the budget. High-fecundity offspring start with less energy and are closer to metabolic death — they must establish an energy income quickly or die. Low-fecundity offspring start with more energy and can weather adverse conditions. Neither strategy dominates — their relative success depends on environmental context. In stable, competitive environments, few well-provisioned offspring outcompete many fragile ones. In disturbed or empty environments, many fragile offspring colonise faster.

**5. Specialist vs. generalist.** A specialist concentrating in one capability can achieve higher net return *in that niche* than a generalist who splits investment across many — but, contrary to a tempting shortcut, superlinear per-trait maintenance is **not** what makes this so. With independent additive convex costs, a generalist at `(t, t)` pays `2·c·t^p` while a specialist at `2t` pays `c·(2t)^p = 2^p·c·t^p`; since `2^p > 2` for `p > 1`, convexity penalises *concentration*, not breadth. The genuine penalties on breadth are **functional incompatibility** (capabilities whose physical demands conflict — autotrophy's substrate-anchored stillness against mobility's movement — cannot both be expressed by one body, regardless of what is paid) and **structural fragility** (trade-off #9: a broad, high-entropy body dies having lost a smaller fraction of its peak structure). These confine generalists without forbidding them — a generalist combining *compatible* capabilities (autotrophy with the sessile form of heterotrophy, the mixotroph) persists in its own niche but does not dominate the specialists' niches. The compatible mixotroph counts as confined while its heterotrophy is conditional on poor ground and it drains a minority of the carcass pile (flow 1). For the autotrophy × heterotrophy pair this term is **specified and latent**: a per-tick maintenance cost `c_AH × (A·H)^(p/2)`, where `A` and `H` are the autotrophy and heterotrophy traits as per-trait maintenance reads them, `p` is the maintenance cost exponent, and `c_AH` is a world parameter. It is zero for a specialist in either trait and smooth everywhere. Its degree is `p`, the same as every per-trait term, for any `p` genesis searches: a fixed-degree product would make the weight of the tax on mixotrophy drift against the other maintenance costs whenever the search moved `p`, coupling two knobs the smooth-landscape constraint keeps independent. At the default `p = 2` it is `c_AH × A × H`. It grows with how much of each machinery is carried, so a trace of heterotrophy on a large photosynthetic investment costs a trace. A steeper form would make modest mixotrophy nearly free and leave untouched the broad middle of producers carrying some heterotrophy, which is where the mixotroph mode lives. It has no structure factor, because per-trait maintenance has none: size is paid for by the structure term, and scaling this one would be a second size penalty paid by mixotrophs alone. It covers this pair only. The autotrophy × mobility conflict is meant to be physical, not a cost (trade-off #2, #648), and the generalised product over every pair would tax combinations the design treats as compatible, such as a mobile hunter's mobility × heterotrophy. The cost works with the retention rule of flow 3, not alone. The deficit term makes a drain's nutrient worth more to a producer the shorter its ground, and the cost is paid wherever the machinery is carried, so heterotrophy pays only where the deficit is large. A cost alone would thin mixotrophy everywhere alike; it would make it rare, not conditional. `c_AH` defaults to 0, so the term is latent and every recipe that does not set it is unchanged by it, as with size-scaled uptake and the network. Genesis searches it on a linear range from 0: the range must hold 0 exactly, which rules out a log scale. Its top is set by measurement, not copied from the per-trait costs. Those are searched over [0.001, 0.1], small against a typical metabolic cost of about 2 per tick, so a cross term on that scale would not bite. The top is the value at which a typical light-fed mixotroph's cross-trait cost is about twice its median drain income, read on #663's atlas (committed until #677, searched before the deficit rule and without `c_AH`) under the flow-3 deficit rule. There, heterotrophy cannot pay for itself unless the deficit makes its nutrient worth something. Genesis does not search it, and its default 0 holds. **6. Mobility buys both reach and awareness.** Sensing range is not independently priced — it is derived from mobility investment. Paying for mobility buys both physical reach and a wider perceptual horizon; investing less in mobility narrows both. There is no separate "sense vs. save" knob: the cost of wider awareness is paid through the superlinear mobility maintenance cost, and the trade-off against saving energy is the same one a sessile strategy already makes against an active one. This is why sessile autotrophs do not need rich perception — without reach, distant information has no actionable value.

**7. Stoichiometric constraint.** Agents need nutrient in amounts determined by their traits — more capable agents demand more nutrient per unit energy. Food sources have varying nutrient-to-energy ratios. An agent consuming nutrient-poor food must eat more to satisfy its nutrient demand, wasting excess energy as heat. An agent consuming nutrient-rich food retains what it needs and excretes the excess nutrient to the available pool. The limiting currency (energy or nutrient) constrains how much of the consumed material the consumer can actually use.

**8. Sexual vs. asexual reproduction.** Sexual reproduction produces more diverse offspring (crossover recombines two parents' traits) but requires a compatible mate — a cost in mate-finding, energy, and the constraint that both parents must be present. Asexual reproduction requires no mate but produces less diverse offspring (parent traits plus mutation only). In dense populations with coevolutionary pressure (predator-prey arms races, competition), variation is valuable and sexual reproduction is favoured. In sparse populations or stable environments, the mate-finding cost outweighs the variation benefit and asexual reproduction is favoured. Which strategy a lineage relies on is emergent, not prescribed.

**9. Complexity vs. structural resilience.** An agent's structural death threshold — the fraction of peak structure below which structural damage is fatal — scales with trait spread. It is *peak-relative*: `threshold = structural_fragility(traits) × peak_structure`, where `structural_fragility` is the normalised entropy of the trait vector (∈ [0, 1): specialist → ~0, uniform generalist → ~1) and `peak_structure` is the agent's developmental high-water mark. Death by structural loss is therefore catabolising your own body past a survivable *fraction of what you built* (a wasting/starvation phenomenon — DEB maintenance cannibalising structure once reserve is gone), not falling below an absolute floor a small newborn never had a chance to clear. An agent with investment concentrated in few traits (specialist) has a simple body plan that tolerates significant structural loss; a moss at half its mass is still a functioning moss. An agent with investment spread across many traits (generalist) has complex, interdependent systems that cannot sustain partial loss — it dies having lost a smaller fraction of its peak (a higher fragility). This makes generalists more vulnerable to consumption — a predator needs to eat less of a complex agent to kill it. This fragility is, together with functional incompatibility (#5), one of the two standing penalties on breadth — and the load-bearing one, since per-trait maintenance is breadth-neutral. Broad trait investment is therefore penalised on the mortality side (fragility) and, where the capabilities physically conflict, on the viability side (incompatibility); the cross-trait interaction maintenance term named in the cost-structure preamble is the reserve lever should these prove too weak.

These trade-offs are the differentiation engine. They do not prescribe what roles emerge — they create the selection pressure that makes role differentiation advantageous. Functional incompatibility and structural fragility guarantee this pressure is always present — it cannot be overcome by acquiring more energy, because it is physical, not budgetary. Superlinear per-trait maintenance shapes how far each trait is pushed; the pressure toward differentiation rather than breadth comes from the other two forces.

## Design constraints on mechanism choice

### Smooth parameter landscape

Every mechanism in the world rules will be placed into a search algorithm (world genesis) that explores parameter space to find initial conditions producing the expected ecological properties. This imposes a meta-constraint on mechanism design: **mechanisms must produce smooth, gradient-rich responses to parameter changes.** Hard thresholds, cliffs, and vast flat regions in parameter space make genesis search intractable — the optimiser needs gradient signal everywhere, not just near the target.

Concretely:
- Prefer continuous costs over binary gates. A trait that becomes gradually less viable as its cost increases is searchable; a trait that is either free or forbidden is not.
- Prefer superlinear scaling over hard ceilings. A cost that grows quadratically with investment creates a smooth trade-off surface; a budget that enforces a hard sum-to-one constraint creates a simplex with edges.
- Prefer independent, individually-tunable knobs over coupled constraints. When one parameter controls multiple trade-offs simultaneously, the search cannot adjust one without disturbing others.
- Avoid mechanisms whose behaviour changes qualitatively at a threshold. Phase transitions in the physics create discontinuities in the fitness landscape that the optimiser must jump across rather than descend.

This constraint does not mean every mechanism must be simple. It means every mechanism must degrade gracefully under perturbation — a small change in parameters should produce a small change in emergent behaviour.

**Known, accepted exception: hard distance predicates.** Feeding contact, light-competition membership and mate-finding reach are step functions of *position*: a target just inside a consumer's reach is drained and one just outside is not; a producer just inside the light-competition radius shades its neighbour and one just outside does not. Outcomes are therefore discontinuous in position at measure-zero boundaries that f32 rounding can reach. The radii themselves are smooth in every parameter, and positions are not searched directly, so this is accepted rather than smoothed. See [Hard distance predicates are an accepted discontinuity in position](#hard-distance-predicates-are-an-accepted-discontinuity-in-position) for the evidence, the consequence for spatial symmetry guarantees, and the alternatives considered.
